A container tiny house is quickly set up – connecting it to power, water and sewer often is not. Where site servicing costs tens of thousands of euros or is impossible, off-grid solutions come into play. Much is technically feasible, but not everything is economically sensible. For the fundamentals of the fit-out, see [Tiny house from a container](/en/ratgeber/tiny-house-aus-container); for the building physics of the shell, see [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen). The connected alternatives are covered in [Power supply for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss) and [Water supply and waste water](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser). This guide reflects the legal situation in Germany.

## What off-grid living realistically means

### Full vs. partial autonomy

**Full autonomy** means: no supply line from outside, all year round, without refuelling or deliveries. This is the most expensive and demanding case. **Partial autonomy** covers most of the demand independently and closes individual gaps via a grid connection, gas bottles, tank refills or a generator. In practice it is almost always the better solution – it drastically reduces both investment and outage risk.

### The winter problem

The decisive factor in Germany is the seasonal distribution of solar irradiation. A PV system often yields only around 10 to 20 percent of a summer month's output in December and January – precisely when heating, hot water and lighting demand peaks. This winter gap cannot be closed with a larger battery, because storage bridges hours to days, not months. Being off-grid in winter requires either a heavily oversized array, a second energy source (wood, gas, generator) or a grid connection.

> Rule of thumb: batteries shift energy across hours, not across seasons. The winter gap is solved with a second energy source, not with more storage.

### Worked example: positive over the year, negative in December

**Assumptions:** one person, frugal, year-round, around 1,500 kWh of electricity per year, heating and hot water not electric. PV system 4 kWp, annual yield 800 to 1,000 kWh per kWp, in December roughly 15 to 25 kWh per kWp (guide values that vary by region and orientation).

| Item | Year | December |
|---|---|---|
| PV yield (4 kWp) | approx. 3,200 – 4,000 kWh | approx. 60 – 100 kWh |
| Demand | approx. 1,500 kWh | approx. 125 kWh (annual average), usually more in winter |
| Balance | clear surplus | shortfall approx. 25 – 65 kWh, more with higher winter demand |

Over the year the array produces more than twice the demand – and still falls short in December. A 10 kWh battery barely helps, because the shortfall builds up over weeks of little sunshine rather than on a single day. An island system cannot store the summer surplus; it is wasted once the battery is full. The calculation shows why a generator, a second heat and energy source or a grid connection should be planned for winter.

## Power: photovoltaics and storage

### Sizing by consumption

As a rule of thumb, **1 kWp of photovoltaics in Germany yields roughly 800 to 1,000 kWh per year**, depending on region, tilt and orientation; modules lying flat on a container roof tend towards the lower end. Roof area is limited: about 14.8 m² (external) on a 20-foot unit and about 29.7 m² on a 40-foot unit – enough for about six or twelve standard modules, i.e. approx. 2.4 or 4.8 kWp. Layout details are in [Photovoltaics on a container](/en/ratgeber/photovoltaik-auf-dem-container). If that is not enough, a ground-mounted array next to the container is needed.

| Use case | Annual consumption (approx.) | PV recommendation | Storage (LiFePO4) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekend use, summer only | 300 – 600 kWh | 1 – 2 kWp | 2 – 5 kWh |
| 1 person, frugal, year-round | 1,200 – 1,800 kWh | 3 – 5 kWp | 5 – 10 kWh |
| 2 people, refrigeration + electronics | 2,000 – 2,800 kWh | 5 – 8 kWp | 10 – 15 kWh |
| 2 people incl. electric hot water | 3,500 – 5,000 kWh | 8 – 12 kWp | 15 – 25 kWh |

Storage is sized against daily consumption: one to two days' worth is the sensible corridor. **LiFePO4 cells** are the standard because they are thermally stable, achieve 3,000 to 6,000 cycles and tolerate deep discharge. The battery must stay frost-free – charging below 0 °C damages the cells unless heating management is built in. Siting, fire safety and insurance questions for battery storage are covered in [Storing batteries and hazardous materials in a container](/en/ratgeber/container-akkus-gefahrstoffe-lagern).

### Island system or grid connection

| Criterion | Island system (off-grid) | Grid-connected |
|---|---|---|
| Investment | high (storage mandatory) | lower |
| Security of supply | weather-dependent | always assured |
| Surplus power | wasted once storage is full | can be exported |
| Approval | no grid registration needed | registration with the network operator |
| Winter operation | second source required | uncritical |

A rough comparison using the guide values from [Power supply for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss): a fixed connection with a newly laid underground cable (approx. €2,000 – 8,000 or more) plus meter cabinet (approx. €800 – 2,500) comes to about €2,800 to 10,500 in total. An island system, by contrast, needs storage and island inverter equipment – approx. €6,000 to 12,000 together according to the cost table below – and still requires an additional source in winter. If a grid connection is achievable for under roughly €10,000, it is therefore usually the better economic choice; the PV system can then run grid-connected. Off-grid pays off mainly where servicing is considerably more expensive or impossible. If you plan a generator: never run combustion engines inside the container or any enclosed space (carbon monoxide).

### Typical loads

The biggest consumers are almost always thermal appliances. An electric instantaneous water heater draws 18 to 27 kW – no ordinary island system can supply that. A refrigerator needs only 100 to 200 kWh per year, LED lighting throughout the container often under 50 kWh. Staggering washing machine, kettle and induction hob instead of running them in parallel allows a smaller inverter.

## Water: cistern, well, drinking water quality

Water demand with a frugal lifestyle is roughly 50 to 90 litres per person per day – well below the German average of about 125 litres, but achievable only with low-flow fittings, short showers and a separating toilet.

**Rainwater** is the obvious source. A 30 m² container roof theoretically captures around 21,000 litres at 700 mm of annual rainfall, realistically 15,000 to 18,000 litres after evaporation and filter losses. A cistern of 3,000 to 6,000 litres bridges typical dry spells. Rainwater is readily suitable for the toilet, washing machine and garden. A 20-foot roof (about 14.8 m²) delivers only around half under the same assumptions, i.e. approx. 7,000 to 9,000 litres.

### Worked example: how far does roof water go?

Assumption: 40-foot roof, realistically 15,000 to 18,000 litres of rainwater per year (700 mm rainfall; regional values are considerably lower or higher).

| Household | Annual demand | Covered by roof water (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 person, 50 l/day | approx. 18,250 l | approx. 80 – 100 % |
| 1 person, 90 l/day | approx. 32,850 l | approx. 45 – 55 % |
| 2 people, 50 l/day | approx. 36,500 l | approx. 40 – 50 % |
| 2 people, 90 l/day | approx. 65,700 l | approx. 23 – 27 % |

Even for two people, the roof of a single container covers only part of the demand. Additional roof areas (carport, shed), a well or regular tank refills close the gap. On cistern size: 5,000 litres last about five weeks without rain at 140 litres a day (two people at 70 l each).

A **well** is more reliable but regulated: the relevant law is the Federal Water Act (WHG) together with the state water acts. Drilling must usually be notified to the lower water authority and, depending on the state, quantity and location (for instance in water protection zones), may also require a permit. Yield and quality depend on the site and cannot be guaranteed in advance.

For **drinking water quality**, a simple filter is not enough. Multi-stage treatment is required: sediment filter, activated carbon and disinfection via UV lamp or reverse osmosis. Regular laboratory testing is decisive; agree frequency and scope with the local health authority (Gesundheitsamt). If your own water is used as drinking water, the German Drinking Water Ordinance (Trinkwasserverordnung) applies and the health authority usually has to be involved. Anyone unwilling to bear that effort should use their own water as service water and cover drinking water via canisters or a small mains connection.

## Waste water and toilets

### Toilet systems

| System | Connection needed | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separating toilet | no | no water, little odour, simple | manual emptying (every 2 – 5 days) |
| Composting toilet | no | on-site composting possible | space required, maturing time, siting rules |
| Chemical toilet | no | cheap, compact | chemicals, disposal only at collection points |
| Conventional (flush) | yes | familiar comfort | water use, sewer or treatment plant needed |

For off-grid containers, the **separating toilet** is the standard: it separates urine and solids, uses no water and needs no waste water connection. That reduces the effluent to pure grey water.

### Grey water and small treatment plants

Grey water from shower, basin and kitchen must not simply be soaked away. Permissible routes are a **small sewage treatment plant** (SBR or fixed-bed system) or a **constructed wetland**. Both require a water-law permit; the lower water authority decides on discharge into soil or a watercourse. The alternative is a sealed cesspit, whose emptying quickly becomes expensive with permanent use – a worked example is in [Water supply and waste water](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser). Small treatment plants additionally require annual servicing by a specialist. Constructed wetlands run largely without electricity but need far more space and react sensitively to fluctuating loads – for instance with weekend-only use.

## Heating and hot water

| Heating type | Suitability in a container | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Wood stove | very good, grid-independent | fire safety critical, chimney sweep sign-off |
| Infrared panels | only with very good insulation | high power demand, barely viable off-grid |
| Gas heating (LPG) | good, high power density | bottle storage, flue routing, CO alarm |
| Split heat pump | very efficient | winter power demand peaks when PV yields least |
| Diesel/air heater | good for small floor areas | tank, noise, regular servicing |

The **wood stove** is the classic route to heat without a grid – but the most delicate in fire safety terms. The steel body and the usually combustible interior lining demand strict clearances to the manufacturer's specification, a non-combustible hearth plate with spark protection and a properly executed wall penetration. Stove and chimney must be signed off by the district chimney sweep; operating without that approval is not permitted. In airtight, well-insulated containers, an external combustion air supply is usually necessary; the chimney sweep determines what applies in the individual case. More on clearances and materials in [Fire safety in containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften).

For **hot water**, the electric instantaneous heater is the most obvious but least suitable option off-grid. Practical alternatives are a gas instantaneous heater with external wall flue, a cylinder with solar thermal collector plus electric backup, or a hot water heat pump where PV surplus is available.

## Cooking and refrigeration

**Induction** is efficient and clean but briefly draws 2 to 3.5 kW – the inverter must deliver it. **Gas** decouples cooking from the electrical system and is the safer route in winter; an 11 kg bottle lasts several months. Adequate ventilation (see [Ventilating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung)) and a CO detector are then necessary; LPG bottles do not belong in enclosed rooms without suitable storage. For refrigeration, compressor units on 12/24 V DC pay off because they avoid the detour via the inverter. Absorption fridges are inefficient in continuous operation.

## Cost overview: two packages

The components can be grouped into two typical packages. **Package A (partial autonomy)** assumes a power and drinking water connection and adds PV, storage, rainwater and a separating toilet. **Package B (full autonomy)** adds a well, water treatment, a small treatment plant and a wood stove to package A.

| Component | Cost (approx.) | Package A | Package B |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 kWp PV system incl. installation | €4,000 – 7,200 | yes | yes |
| 10 kWh battery storage (LiFePO4) | €4,000 – 8,000 | yes | yes |
| Inverter, charge controller, distribution | €2,000 – 4,000 | yes | yes |
| 5,000 l cistern incl. installation | €3,000 – 6,000 | yes | yes |
| Separating toilet | €700 – 1,500 | yes | yes |
| Water treatment incl. UV | €1,500 – 4,000 | – | yes |
| Well (drilling, permit-dependent) | €3,000 – 10,000 | – | yes |
| Small treatment plant (purchase and installation) | €5,000 – 15,000 | – | yes |
| Wood stove incl. chimney and sign-off | €2,500 – 6,000 | – | yes |
| **Total** | | **approx. €13,700 – 26,700** | **approx. €25,700 – 61,700** |

Not included: grid and water connection costs for package A, generator and fuel, permit fees, laboratory analyses and servicing. For genuine full autonomy in winter, a 4 kWp roof is usually not enough (see the worked example above); extra modules on a frame or carport push package B higher still. The PV range corresponds to roughly €1,000 to 1,800 per kWp as in [Photovoltaics on a container](/en/ratgeber/photovoltaik-auf-dem-container); the treatment plant range is taken from [Water supply and waste water](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser).

All figures are rough guide ranges without guarantee (as of 2026) and not binding prices. They vary considerably with location, ground conditions, official requirements and the share of own labour. For a reliable calculation, obtain several current quotes. The overall cost of a container tiny house is discussed in [Container tiny house: costs](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten).

## Decision aid: which route fits?

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Grid connection achievable for under approx. €10,000 | connect to the grid, add package A |
| Summer or weekend use only | small island system (1 – 2 kWp), water tank, separating toilet; drain the container for winter |
| Year-round, servicing very expensive or impossible | package B with second heat source, generator and reserves |
| Drinking water from own well planned | ask the water authority and health authority first, wait for test drilling and analysis |
| Waste water cannot be discharged | ask the authority about a treatment plant or cesspit before settling on the site |

Frost protection for seasonal use is covered in [Containers in winter](/en/ratgeber/container-im-winter).

## Legal pitfalls

- **Wells:** subject to notification and frequently to permit by the lower water authority.
- **Small treatment plants and constructed wetlands:** water-law permit required, servicing obligation.
- **Chimney and fireplace:** sign-off by the district chimney sweep, regular sweeping.
- **Well water as drinking water:** observe the Drinking Water Ordinance, involve the health authority.
- **Building law:** autonomy does not replace a building permit. A permanently occupied container is a structural installation; whether and which approval is needed depends on the state building code, location, duration and use, and in undesignated outlying areas also on § 35 of the Federal Building Code (BauGB). See [Building permits for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and [Tiny house: plot and legal issues](/en/ratgeber/tiny-house-stellplatz-und-recht).
- **Compulsory connection and use:** many municipalities mandate connection to the public water supply and sewer. Exemption is possible but must be applied for case by case.

## Important note

This guide gives a general overview and does not replace professional planning or legal advice. Electrical installation, battery storage, gas systems and fireplaces belong in the hands of qualified specialists. Requirements for wells, waste water and drinking water differ between German states and municipalities; the information given by the competent authorities is binding. All figures are guide values without guarantee.

## Conclusion

Off-grid living in a container works – but not as a budget solution. The toilet and part of the water demand can be handled independently with comparatively little effort; the power system alone costs roughly €10,000 to 19,200 in package A, and things become expensive and complicated above all with waste water, drinking water quality and energy supply in winter. Anyone with the choice should lay the grid connection and combine it with PV, storage, a separating toilet and rainwater use: this noticeably reduces consumption and running costs without sacrificing comfort in January. Genuine full autonomy makes sense where servicing is simply not possible – and should then be planned from the outset with reserves, a second heat source and a realistic maintenance budget.
