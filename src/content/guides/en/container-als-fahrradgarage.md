Bicycles, and e-bikes in particular, need a safe, dry place. The cellar often lacks the room, bike stands in the yard are hardly theft-proof, and a brick garage is expensive. A container is an obvious solution: lockable, weatherproof, quick to set up and movable later. Whether it pays off depends on a few questions: how many bikes must fit, how do they get in, and what happens to the batteries? This guide works through it using a 20 ft container as the example. The legal remarks refer to German law. The basics of using a container for storage are in [Using a storage container properly](/en/ratgeber/lagercontainer-richtig-nutzen).

## The dimensions you calculate with

A standard 20 ft container measures roughly 5.90 m long, 2.35 m wide and 2.39 m high inside, with a high cube about 2.70 m high. The door opening at the end is about 2.34 m wide and 2.28 m high (high cube about 2.58 m). The values, together with other types, are in [Container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse).

Bicycles (rough guide values, models vary):

| Bike | Length | Handlebar width | Remark |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| City or trekking bike | approx. 1.70–1.90 m | approx. 0.55–0.70 m | Standard |
| E-bike | as city bike | as city bike | approx. 25 kg, lifting is difficult |
| Children's and youth bike | approx. 1.10–1.60 m | approx. 0.45–0.55 m | – |
| Cargo bike (long john) | approx. 2.3–2.6 m | approx. 0.70–0.90 m | Only sensible at floor level |

The container's width is decisive: a 1.85 m bike does fit across the container (2.35 m), but only about 0.5 m of width is left – too little for an aisle. That determines the possible layouts.

## Three layouts compared

**Layout A – bikes lengthways along both walls.** The bikes stand one behind the other along the long wall, front wheel towards the door. At 1.85 m per bike, three fit per wall (3 × 1.85 m = 5.55 m of 5.90 m). That gives **6 spaces**. With 2 × 0.65 m taken up, an aisle of 2.35 − 1.30 = about 1.05 m remains in the middle. Advantage: every bike can be rolled out individually, and cargo bikes fit; drawback: few spaces.

**Layout B – wall mounts with the front wheel raised.** The bikes hang or stand angled to the wall, alternately high and low. The spacing between two bikes drops to about 0.40 m. On a 5.90 m wall that gives 14 spaces per side on paper. Deducting corners and the door area leaves about 12 per side in practice, i.e. **up to about 24 spaces**. Depending on the mount, the bikes project roughly 0.6–0.8 m into the room, and the central aisle shrinks to about 0.8–1.1 m. Raising a 1.85 m bike needs a room height of about 2 m; that just works in a standard container (2.39 m), with more margin in a high cube.

**Layout C – mixed.** A cargo bike and a trailer stand at floor level at the back, with wall mounts for ordinary bikes in front. That is practical for families and businesses with a mixed fleet.

| Layout | Spaces (approx.) | Suitable for | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: lengthways, floor level | 6 | Families, e-bikes, cargo bike | Few spaces |
| B: wall mounts, raised | up to approx. 24 | Apartment blocks, clubs, schools | Lifting e-bikes (approx. 25 kg) is awkward; special mounts with lifting aid cost more |
| C: mixed | depending on share | Households with a cargo bike | Needs planning |

The figures are theoretical upper limits. Draw your own fleet to scale before ordering, including handlebar and pedal positions – pedals and handlebars are what cost space in practice.

## Cost per space

A worked example for orientation, based on the prices in [What does a container cost?](/en/ratgeber/was-kostet-ein-container): a used 20 ft storage container costs approx. €1,500–3,000, delivery approx. €150–500 per trip, crane placement approx. €100–300. Together that is approx. €1,750–3,800, excluding foundation, ramp and fittings.

- With 6 spaces (layout A): approx. €290–630 per space.
- With 12 spaces (assumed, layout B): approx. €145–315 per space.

Add fittings (mounts, lighting, lock, ventilation) and, where needed, foundation and access. The amounts are orientation values without guarantee; rented containers are an option too (see [Buy or rent a container?](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufen-oder-mieten)).

## Access: threshold, ramp, door

By design the container floor sits slightly above the ground; depending on the foundation you get a threshold of roughly 10–20 cm to overcome. With a ramp of about 10 % gradient, a 15 cm threshold needs about 1.5 m of ramp (0.15 m ÷ 0.10). For higher installations the ramp gets longer. If you need step-free use, plan for it.

The container floor is made of wood-based board or hardwood and can be slippery when wet. Used floors may be treated with preservatives; see [Repairing a container floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen). A non-slip covering – for example grid mats – helps and protects the floor from tyre wear and dirt.

On the door: with the standard double door, the leaves can usually be opened wide against the side wall (often up to about 270°), and the opening is then about 2.34 m wide. That is enough for cargo bikes. A side door (on open-side types, see [Open-side container](/en/container/open-side-container)) only makes sense if there is room in front of the long side.

## E-bike batteries: charging, storing, fire safety

Bicycle garages today nearly always hold e-bikes, which makes the battery a safety topic. Some principles that follow from typical manufacturer guidance:

- **Do not charge unattended**, use only the charger belonging to the battery and tested sockets.
- **Mind the temperature.** An uninsulated container can heat up strongly in summer and cool down strongly in winter. The manufacturer's limits for storage and charging temperature are part of the planning; some manufacturers advise charging and storing batteries inside a building.
- **Smoke alarm** or a heat detector and a fire extinguisher within reach make sense. Which obligations apply depends on use and state law (see [Fire protection in containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften)).
- **Electrical installation by a specialist.** Sockets and supply cable belong in professional hands, see [Power supply for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss) and [Electrical planning for the interior](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau).
- **Clarify insurance.** Whether bikes and batteries in a container are covered by household or business insurance depends on the policy terms; ask before buying (see [Insuring a container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern)).

## Moisture and ventilation

Wet bikes and rain gear bring moisture into a steel volume of about 33 m³. The consequences are condensation on the ceiling and rust on bikes, chains and the container. At least two opposite ventilation openings improve air exchange; background in [Ventilating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung). Wipe bikes dry before they go in rather than parking them dripping.

## Theft protection

A container full of bikes is an attractive target because several valuable bikes can be taken at once. A high-quality lock (cross bar or lock box), anchoring the bikes to the floor and good visibility of the site protect more than any extra effort inside. The measures are detailed in [Securing a container: theft protection](/en/ratgeber/container-sichern-diebstahlschutz). It is also worth noting down the frame numbers of all bikes.

## Legal questions

- **Building law.** Whether a container as a bicycle garage is exempt from permission depends on the federal state, location, size, duration and use; the state building codes are decisive. Check with the building authority before ordering and read [Containers and building permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and [Boundary distances and neighbour law](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand).
- **Tenancy and owners' associations.** Tenants generally need the landlord's consent; with condominium ownership the owners' community must be involved.
- **Parking obligations.** Some state building codes and local by-laws require bicycle parking for certain buildings. Whether a container meets this obligation is decided by the authority case by case.
- **Public land.** If the container stands on a pavement or street, a special-use permit is needed (see [Containers on public land](/en/ratgeber/container-oeffentlicher-grund-sondernutzung)).

## Checklist before ordering

1. Record number and type of bikes (e-bike, cargo bike, trailer) and draw them to scale.
2. Choose layout A, B or C and recalculate the number of spaces.
3. Site: level ground, access for lorry and crane, ramp length, visibility.
4. Battery concept: where is charging done, at what temperature, with which detector?
5. Plan ventilation and floor covering.
6. Clarify building law and consent (landlord, community, neighbours).
7. Decide on insurance and lock.

## Important note

This article is general orientation and not legal or fire-safety advice. The dimensions of bicycles are guide values; cost figures are orientation values without guarantee. The specifications of the bicycle, battery and mount manufacturers and of the competent authority take precedence.

## Conclusion

Depending on layout, a 20 ft container holds roughly 6 to about 24 bicycles – the number depends less on the container than on bikes, mounts and room to manoeuvre. At about €145–630 per space it is economically manageable as long as no elaborate fit-out is added. Three things matter: access without a struggle, a thought-through battery concept and a site that has been cleared under building law.
