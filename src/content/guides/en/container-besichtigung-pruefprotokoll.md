Buying a used container means buying a piece of steel with a history. Photos in an advert show the best angle – not the underside of the floor, not the door seals, not the smell. That is why the on-site visit is the most important step in the purchase decision. The general checklist and the condition grades are covered in [Buying a Used Container](/en/ratgeber/gebrauchten-container-kaufen). This guide goes one step further: it describes the **routine of a viewing**, gives you a **protocol with a traffic-light rating**, and shows how to turn the result into a price and a contract.

## Preparation: what to clarify beforehand

A good viewing starts before the appointment. Ask in writing for:

- the **container number** (eleven characters) – you can check beforehand whether it is plausible; see [Reading the Container Number and CSC Plate](/en/ratgeber/container-nummer-csc-plakette)
- year of manufacture, previous owner and previous use (sea transport or stationary)
- the **exact location** where you may inspect it, and whether the container is accessible that day (not blocked in, not stacked)
- whether the container is **loaded** – empty, it is far easier to inspect
- the **agreed price** and whether it includes delivery

Book the appointment in **daylight and dry weather** if you can. Rain shows leaks more easily, but sunshine shows light coming through holes – a second visit can be worthwhile if the first impression is uncertain.

## Equipment for the visit

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Torch (bright) | Interior, underside of the floor, corners |
| Tape measure (at least 5 m) | Checking dimensions |
| Screwdriver or pen | Gently probing rust spots (without damaging) |
| Smartphone | Photos, notes, possibly video |
| Work gloves | Sharp edges, rusty areas |
| Spirit level or level app | Detecting distortion and tilt |
| Printed protocol (see below) | Ticking off in a structured way |
| A companion | A second person helps check; a witness in case of dispute |

For larger investments, bring someone with relevant expertise, such as a person from metalworking or the steel trade. An expert costs money, but can make sense for a purchase in the four-figure range, especially if the container is to be converted.

## The routine in seven steps

### 1. The overall view first

Step back a few metres and look at the container as a whole. Does it stand level? Are the edges parallel, or does one corner look lowered? Visible distortion points to overloading, lifting damage or accident damage.

### 2. Papers and markings

Compare the number on the container with the number in the offer – on the end wall, on the side and on the plate. All places should show the same number. If parts are painted over or replaced, the seller should be able to explain why. The CSC plate states the date of manufacture and so the real age.

### 3. Outer skin

Walk slowly around the container, including the rear and end walls. Look for:

- **surface rust** (superficial, can be treated with paint) and **rust spots or blisters under the paint**, which often point to rust beneath
- **perforation**, i.e. holes or soft spots in the sheet steel
- **dents**, especially in the corner areas and on the corrugations; deep dents may have cracked the steel
- **welds and patches**, which indicate earlier repairs
- the lower **foot areas** of the walls, where splash water and moisture attack most

### 4. Corner fittings and frame

The eight corner fittings are the nodes of the load-bearing structure of corner posts and longitudinal rails. Check each one for cracks, deformation, worn openings and rust. Damaged fittings make lifting and stacking considerably harder. The corner posts and rails should also be straight and undamaged.

### 5. Roof

If it can be done safely, look at the roof from a ladder or with a telescopic pole and photo. Standing water or water marks on the roof indicate dents where water collects. Climbing onto a container roof only makes sense with suitable fall protection; many dealers refuse it anyway because the sheet is not designed for point loads. If in doubt, skip it.

### 6. Doors, seals, locking gear

Open and close both door leaves **several times yourself**. The locking rods must turn, the cams must engage, and the doors must close cleanly against the stop. The rubber seals should be continuous, elastic and unbroken. Also look for **play in the door hinges** and whether the doors sag when open.

### 7. Interior: light, floor, smell

Close the doors without letting the locking rods engage, and keep a second person at the door – **never lock yourself in**, as locked doors usually cannot be opened from inside. Wait until your eyes adjust to the dark: every point of light is a leak. Then sweep the walls, ceiling and floor with the torch. The floor is normally plywood panels about 28 mm thick (hardwood, sometimes bamboo) on steel cross members; it must not be soft, swollen or worn through. Smell the interior: a musty smell suggests moisture, a chemical smell suggests a previous cargo. Ask what was transported before.

## Measuring: do the dimensions fit?

A standard 20-foot container measures about 6.06 × 2.44 m outside, with a height of about 2.59 m (high cube about 2.90 m). Inside it is about 5.90 × 2.35 m with an internal height of about 2.39 m (high cube about 2.70 m). The door opening is about 2.34 m wide and 2.28 m high (high cube about 2.58 m). If your measurements deviate clearly, this may indicate distortion or a different type. Reference values and further types are in [Container Sizes and Dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse).

A simple check for distortion: measure both **diagonals** of the door opening and of the end wall. In an undistorted frame they are almost the same length. If they differ by more than a few centimetres, the frame is deformed, which normally cannot be repaired economically.

## The inspection protocol with traffic-light rating

Copy this table into a document or print it. For each item, assign a colour: **green** (fine), **amber** (defect, fixable, negotiable) or **red** (deal-breaker or expensive defect).

| Check item | Green | Amber | Red |
|---|---|---|---|
| Container number, plate | Consistent, legible, plausible | Partly painted over | Numbers do not match |
| Overall stance | Level, no tilt | Minor dents | Visible distortion |
| Outer skin | Surface rust only | Rust spots, blisters, small patches | Perforation, extensive repairs |
| Corner fittings, frame | Undamaged | Light scratches, surface rust | Cracks, deformation, perforation |
| Roof | Flat, dry | Light dents, no standing water | Puddles, rust, holes |
| Doors, locking gear | Smooth, closing cleanly | Stiff, adjustable | Distorted, jamming, defective |
| Seals | Elastic, continuous | Brittle, partly defective (replaceable) | Missing, largely destroyed |
| Light test inside | Dark | Single light points at seals | Holes in steel or walls |
| Floor | Firm, dry | Stains, minor damage | Soft, worn through, mould |
| Smell | Neutral | Slightly musty | Chemical or strongly mouldy |
| Dimensions, diagonals | Within tolerance | Slight deviation | Clear deviation |

**Rule of thumb for the result:** a single red in the frame, corner fittings, distortion or perforation is normally a reason to reject the purchase or demand a clearly different offer. Several ambers in doors, seals and floor, by contrast, are often a bargaining point, because they can be fixed.

## From finding to price

A defect is worth what its repair costs – plus effort and risk. Work it out for every amber line. The following example uses **assumed values**; actual prices depend on region, material and effort.

| Finding | Remedy | Indicative value (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Brittle door seals | Replace seal set | €100–250 |
| Rust spots on a wall | Remove rust, primer, paint | €100–400 |
| Individual light points | Seal, weld spot | €50–200 |
| Damaged floor panel (part area) | Partial replacement | €150–500 |
| Distorted door leaves | Straighten or replace | varies widely |

Example: the container is offered at €2,400. You find brittle seals and a few rust spots, so roughly €200–650 of effort. A reduction of €300–500 would then be soundly justified, because you have documented and priced the defect. The better your photos and figures, the harder it is to wave away. For the negotiation and for placing the price, see [Comparing Offers](/en/ratgeber/container-angebote-vergleichen) and [What Does a Container Cost?](/en/ratgeber/was-kostet-ein-container). The prices given are German market orientation values without guarantee.

It is different with defects that are not worth repairing. A distorted frame or perforated foot areas are not grounds for a discount but for walking away. This applies all the more if you intend to convert the container: what is tolerable for storage becomes a problem once insulation and fit-out are added.

## Putting the result into the contract

A protocol is only useful if it becomes part of the purchase.

- **Both parties sign** the protocol with date and time; each gets a copy.
- Write the **container number** into the protocol and the purchase contract.
- The **description of condition** in the contract ("wind- and watertight", "floor firm") should match your observations and not be worded more generally.
- Record agreed **repairs** or price reductions in writing.
- **On delivery**, check the container again before the haulier leaves: it must be the same container you viewed.

For the legal position on used containers, warranty rights and the difference between dealer and private seller under German law, see [Purchase Contract and Warranty](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufvertrag-gewaehrleistung). Scams in which a viewing is deliberately prevented are described in [Fake Container Dealers and Advance-Payment Fraud](/en/ratgeber/fake-containerhaendler-vorkasse-betrug).

## If a viewing is not possible

Sometimes the container is far away or in a port depot. In that case:

- **A live video walk-through** instead of prepared pictures: have them show you the places you name ("now the right door from inside, torch on, please").
- **Current dated photos** showing the container number marking and the plate.
- **Commission an inspector on site**, such as an independent surveyor.
- **No advance payment of the full price** before you have seen the container or have a verifiable delivery commitment.

If the seller is unwilling to accept any of these, ask yourself why.

## Common mistakes at the visit

- **Looking only at the outside.** Most problems arise at the floor, at the seals and in the corners.
- **Letting the seller show you the inside instead of opening it yourself.**
- **Going without notes and photos.** Two days later your memory has gaps.
- **Deciding under time pressure.** "Another buyer is coming today" is a familiar sales tactic.
- **Not carrying the protocol into the contract.**

## Important note

This guide is for general orientation and replaces neither a professional survey nor legal advice. Dimensions are standard values and may differ for individual manufacturers. All prices are indicative values without guarantee. For high-priced containers or those intended for living use, an independent survey is recommended.

## Conclusion

A good viewing follows a fixed routine: overall impression, markings, outer skin, frame, roof, doors, interior, dimensions. The traffic-light protocol turns impressions into provable findings. Red points end the negotiation, amber ones make it concrete, and what you record in writing remains available to you later as evidence. Anyone who proceeds like this pays for the condition they actually get.
