Anyone who has ever put up a marquee at four in the morning in the rain immediately understands the appeal of containers at events. A bar container arrives fully fitted, is set down, connected – and is ready to trade an hour later. For festivals, town fairs, sporting events and pop-up hospitality, the container has moved from stopgap to deliberate design choice.

## Why containers work at events

Five properties set them apart from marquees, pagodas and catering trailers:

- **Set-up speed.** Deliver, set down, open the hatch. Instead of floors, poles and fabric you need a crane or hook-lift and a practised crew.
- **Weather resistance.** Steel copes with storms, heavy rain and heat. When a weather warning comes in, trading can often continue where marquees have to be cleared – the organiser decides based on conditions and the safety concept.
- **Overnight security.** Locked steel doors protect stock, till and equipment – a tangible cost advantage at multi-day events.
- **Transportability and reuse.** The same unit can travel to five or six events in a season. That pays off faster than a bespoke stand build.
- **Visual impact.** A wrapped or painted container is an eye-catcher, a photo backdrop and a landmark on site.

There is also an underrated point: containers are modular. Two units side by side create a double service point; three in a U-shape create a sheltered service zone with an inner yard.

## Types of deployment

| Use | Container type | Particularity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bar container | 10 or 20 ft with lifting hatch | Hatch doubles as canopy; counter, dispense system, sink and chilling required |
| Serving point / food stand | 10 ft with serving window | Short routes, hygienic separation of preparation and sales |
| Full kitchen | 20 ft kitchen container | Extraction, grease trap, hand wash basin, heat-resistant surfaces |
| Beverage and stock chilling | Reefer 10–40 ft | Continuous power supply essential, temperature log recommended |
| Sanitary | WC/shower container, partly accessible | Fresh and waste water or tank solution, plan cleaning intervals |
| Backstage / dressing room | Office container with windows | Heating, mirrors, sockets, lockable |
| Ticket and info point | 10 ft with counter window | Position ahead of access control, lighting and network |
| Storage and empties | Standard shipping container | Close to the service point but outside visitor flows |
| Crew and first aid | Office container, ideally with lobby | Keep access clear for emergency vehicles |

For smaller events a bar container, a reefer and one sanitary unit are often enough. With larger visitor numbers it pays to split preparation, service and storage consistently across separate units; there is no fixed threshold – it follows from queues and walking routes.

## Planning technical requirements realistically

The most common planning error is an undersized power connection. Dispense systems, refrigeration, coffee machines and lighting add up quickly.

| Trade | Typical demand | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power, bar container | approx. 8–16 kW | 32 A CEE, separate circuit for dispense and chilling |
| Power, kitchen container | approx. 20–40 kW | 63 A CEE, check appliance ratings; gas is an alternative with extra conditions |
| Power, reefer | approx. 5–12 kW connected load | Continuous supply, including overnight; failure means stock loss. Energy use is much lower, see [running costs](/en/ratgeber/kuehlcontainer-betrieb-stromkosten) |
| Water | approx. 20–60 l per operating hour per bar | Drinking-water hose or tank, backflow preventer |
| Waste water | matches fresh water volume | Collection tank or connection; never discharge to ground |
| Kitchen ventilation | extraction sized to appliance heat | Exhaust direction away from visitor areas |
| Lighting | approx. 300–600 lx in work areas | Plus emergency and route lighting |
| Network / till | LTE router or cable | Card payment needs a stable link; plan a backup |

Allow roughly 20 to 30 percent power headroom. A standby generator as a fallback is not a luxury where cold chains are involved – it is risk management.

### Worked example: power for a town fair

Assume (illustrative values taken from the table): a bar container at 12 kW, a 20 ft reefer at 8 kW connected load, and a serving point with lighting at 6 kW. Together that is 26 kW. With 25 percent headroom: 26 × 1.25 = **32.5 kW**.

A 32 A CEE connection (400 V three-phase) delivers √3 × 400 V × 32 A ≈ 22 kW, a 63 A connection ≈ 43.6 kW. The 32 A connection is therefore not enough in this example unless the loads demonstrably do not run at the same time; a 63 A connection would have room to spare. Whether simultaneity may be assumed is for a qualified electrician to decide, not a rule of thumb. Basics on connections are in [Power supply for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).

The reefer uses far less than its connected load suggests: for chilling in summer heat the reefer guide works with approx. 8–20 kWh per day, so approx. 24–60 kWh for a weekend (3 days). At €0.30/kWh that is approx. €7–18. Connected load determines cable size and fusing, not the electricity bill.

> Practical rule: the distribution board belongs somewhere sheltered but permanently accessible. Cable ramps across visitor routes are mandatory, not optional.

## Set-up and logistics

- **Delivery.** A 20 ft container needs a truck with a crane or a hook-lift vehicle. Check access width, turning circle, ground bearing capacity and height restrictions such as trees and overhead lines.
- **Crane clearance.** The space above the drop point must be free. Overhead cables, marquee roofs and truss structures block the lift more often than expected.
- **Standing area.** Load-bearing, level ground; on grass use track panels or timber boards. Keep the door and hatch sides clear.
- **Sequence.** Sanitary and storage first, then bar and kitchen, decoration last. Connect services before surrounding stands go up – otherwise nobody can reach the distribution board.
- **Schedule.** Rule of thumb for a mid-sized event: one day for delivery and placement, one day for services and fit-out, half a day for inspection and a trial run. Derig takes roughly two thirds of the build time.
- **Derig.** Agree cleaning, empties and waste water disposal contractually in advance, otherwise Monday gets expensive.

## Permits and requirements

The following points are orientation for Germany; the requirements of the responsible local authorities always take precedence (see also the note below).

- **Use of public space.** Squares, streets and parks normally require a special-use permit from the municipality – with several weeks of lead time; details in [Containers on public land](/en/ratgeber/container-oeffentlicher-grund-sondernutzung).
- **Assembly venue rules.** Above certain visitor numbers and areas, additional requirements apply; a safety concept is usually demanded.
- **Food hygiene.** Hand wash basin with hot water, smooth washable surfaces, separated clean and unclean areas, temperature documentation, staff training records.
- **Fire safety.** Keep escape and rescue routes clear, extinguishers per unit, spacing between containers, special rules where gas is used; see [Fire safety for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften).
- **Structural stability.** Stacked containers, roof structures or advertising surfaces may require proof of stability.
- **Noise and operating hours.** Refrigeration units run through the night – a frequent point of conflict near housing.

An early meeting with the licensing office, food inspectorate and fire authority clarifies conditions before they block the schedule. For fixed, longer-term installations building law also comes into play – that depends on location, duration and use, see [Containers and building permits](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

## Practical tips

- **Two service points instead of one long counter.** Queues disperse better across parallel points than along counter length.
- **Plan empties logistics from the start.** A full empties area halts trading faster than an empty chiller.
- **Test the workflow.** A trial run with the real crew reliably exposes missing sockets and cramped routes.
- **Signage legible from a distance.** Offer and prices belong above the hatch, not on the counter.
- **Overnight securing.** Stock and till inside, hatch down, padlock on – the biggest advantage over a marquee.
- **Documentation.** Photos of the condition at handover and return prevent arguments about damage.

## Conclusion

Containers are strong at events when they are brought into the planning early. The units themselves are rarely the problem – bottlenecks arise around power capacity, site access, crane clearance and permit deadlines. Separate the functions cleanly, calculate technical demand with headroom, plan the logistics on a realistic timeline and talk to the authorities early, and you get an infrastructure that is weatherproof, secure and visually distinctive – and ready to roll out again next season.
