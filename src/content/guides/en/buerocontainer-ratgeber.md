When space is short and time is pressing, the office container is often the most pragmatic answer: a module is in place within a day, usable once power is connected, and can be relocated or sold later. The market is hard to survey, though – the range runs from a simple site office to a two-storey unit with corridor, server room and air conditioning. This guide shows what defines an office container technically, which sizes and equipment levels are usual, what to watch for in the energy standard, and what costs to expect.

## What defines an office container

An office container is a **modular room container** – a prefabricated building module, not a converted shipping container. The load-bearing element is a welded steel frame with corner posts, into which insulated sandwich panels for wall, roof and floor are fitted. Inside, walls are lined with coated boards and the floor gets a hard-wearing covering.

Typical features distinguishing it from other container variants:

- **Double-glazed windows** with turn-and-tilt fittings, usually plus shutters or grilles
- **Complete electrical installation** ex works: sub-distribution with RCD, sockets, ceiling lights, external connection
- **Heating** as an electric convector or wall heater with room thermostat
- **Insulated steel exterior door** with a security lock
- **Coupling capability**: connecting fittings and prepared wall openings for larger units

The decisive difference from a shipping container lies in building physics. An office container is designed for people to occupy: closed insulation layer, defined thermal transmittance values, daylight and a ventilation option. A converted shipping container can do the same, but takes far more effort – see [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen).

## Sizes and floor areas

Office containers follow a module grid with roughly 2.44 m exterior width. Length and coupling produce practically any floor area.

| Module | Exterior approx. | Usable area approx. | Realistic workplaces | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 foot | 3.00 × 2.44 m | 6.5 – 7 m² | 1 | Gatehouse, kiosk, single desk |
| 15 foot | 4.50 × 2.44 m | 10 – 11 m² | 1 – 2 | Single office, small site office |
| 20 foot | 6.06 × 2.44 m | 13.5 – 14.5 m² | 2 | Standard office, site management |
| 2 × 20 foot | 6.06 × 4.88 m | 27 – 29 m² | 4 – 5 | Team office with meeting corner |
| 4 × 20 foot | 6.06 × 9.76 m | 55 – 58 m² | 8 – 10 | Administration unit with corridor |
| 8 × 20 foot, 2 storeys | 12.12 × 9.76 m | 110 – 116 m² | 16 – 20 | Temporary office building |

These workplace figures are practical and deliberately sit below the theoretical maximum. Workplace regulations impose requirements on circulation space, room height and daylight for permanent office workplaces; during construction these are relaxed, for permanent administrative use they are not. When in doubt, allow 8 to 10 m² per workplace including circulation.

## Equipment levels

Suppliers usually distinguish three fit-out levels. Labels vary, contents are similar.

| Feature | Basic | Standard | Comfort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Insulation | Basic, seasonal use | Increased thickness, year-round | Enhanced package, low U-value |
| Windows | 1 window, insulating glass | 2 windows with shutters | Several windows, solar control glass |
| Electrics | Basic sockets | Multiple sockets, network outlets | Cable trunking, server feed, UPS provision |
| Heating | Convector, 2 kW | Thermostat-controlled, several units | Split unit with heating function |
| Air conditioning | Not included | Prepared/optional | Standard |
| Floor | PVC | Reinforced PVC | Needle felt or design covering |
| Security | Standard lock | Security lock, window grilles | Alarm provision, security door |

The basic level suffices for short projects in the warm season. As soon as work runs year-round, stepping up to standard almost always pays – the extra cost is often recovered through heating savings within two or three winters.

## Technology in detail

**Electrics and network:** allow at least four sockets and one network outlet per workplace. More important than the number is the distribution: socket strips near the floor on a single wall almost inevitably lead to cable clutter. A continuous dado trunking is far better. The external connection uses a CEE plug (16 A or 32 A); with several modules a shared main distribution board makes sense.

**Heating:** electric convectors are standard because they need no infrastructure. A rough figure is 60 to 100 W per square metre, depending on insulation and window area. In continuous operation and with high electricity prices, split units with a heat pump function are more economical, since one kilowatt hour yields several times as much heat.

**Air conditioning and ventilation:** containers have an unfavourable ratio of envelope surface to volume and heat up quickly in summer. External solar shading, a light-coloured roof coating and a split unit are the effective levers. Window ventilation suffices for two people; from about four people in the room, mechanical ventilation or a unit with heat recovery is noticeably more pleasant.

**Lighting:** LED panels providing around 500 lux at the desk are standard. Look for glare-free versions and a light colour around 4000 K – the warm white luminaires often fitted quickly feel tiring in offices.

## Insulation and energy

Insulation quality determines comfort and running costs. Typical guide values for thermal transmittance:

| Component | Basic U-value approx. | Standard U-value approx. | Comfort/energy package approx. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| External wall | 0.55 – 0.70 W/(m²·K) | 0.35 – 0.45 W/(m²·K) | 0.20 – 0.30 W/(m²·K) |
| Roof | 0.45 – 0.60 W/(m²·K) | 0.30 – 0.40 W/(m²·K) | 0.18 – 0.25 W/(m²·K) |
| Floor | 0.55 – 0.70 W/(m²·K) | 0.35 – 0.45 W/(m²·K) | 0.25 – 0.32 W/(m²·K) |
| Windows | 2.6 – 3.0 W/(m²·K) | 1.3 – 1.6 W/(m²·K) | 0.9 – 1.2 W/(m²·K) |

These are guide values and vary by manufacturer and build-up. For permanent, approval-bound use as a building, statutory requirements may demand considerably stricter values than the basic version delivers – a common stumbling block when converting a site office into permanent office space.

Also watch thermal bridges: steel frame, corner posts and coupling joints conduct heat far better than the panel surface. In practice this shows as condensation in the corners. Thermally broken profiles, careful joint insulation and adequate ventilation help.

## Coupling and multi-storey units

Larger areas are created by coupling modules. Containers placed side by side are joined and braced via corner fittings; if partition walls are to be removed, a structurally verified beam replaces the load-bearing wall. This is where quality shows: removing walls afterwards without verification jeopardises the stackability of the whole unit.

Two-storey units are standard and usually approved up to three storeys. Required in that case are:

- External staircase with landing and railings, or an internal stair module
- Escape route concept with two exits above a certain occupancy
- Higher foundation requirements, because point loads rise considerably
- Narrower corridor modules or half modules for circulation

For larger units, assembly is a substantial share – depending on scope, allow roughly 15 to 30 percent of the module price for erection, coupling and services. See also [Buying or renting a container](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufen-oder-mieten).

## Uses beyond the construction site

Office containers left the building site long ago. Common applications include:

- **Sales and service:** sales pavilions, show offices in new developments, workshop reception, vehicle handover
- **Education:** interim classrooms during refurbishment, care rooms, staff rooms
- **Administration:** expansions during staff growth, reception and gatehouse buildings, test centres
- **Events:** production offices, accreditation, ticketing, security control rooms

These uses usually face higher demands on appearance, accessibility and energy standard than a construction site. Anyone planning publicly accessible rooms should factor in ramps, door widths and lighting from the outset.

## Approval

An office container erected permanently and intended for people to occupy is generally a structure under building law – regardless of being movable. Building application, type of use and separation distances therefore matter. Relief applies to construction-period use within the approved site. Details in [Container building permit](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

## Checklist before purchase

- How many workplaces are needed, and how much area per place is realistic?
- Year-round use? Then choose standard or comfort insulation and get U-values confirmed in writing.
- What is the power supply – site distribution board, mains connection, capacity in amperes?
- Are network, telephony and server location clarified?
- Is air conditioning needed, or do solar shading plus ventilation suffice?
- Should modules be coupled or stacked? Clarify structural design and manufacturer clearance.
- Is access suitable for lorries, and is a crane required?
- Approval situation checked – construction-period use or permanent structure?
- When renting: contract term, notice period, return condition and maintenance duties.

## Conclusion

Office containers are a mature, calculable solution for additional workplaces – provided the requirements are defined properly in advance. The three levers with the greatest influence on satisfaction and cost are insulation standard, electrical equipment and summer heat protection. Choosing the basic version here saves on purchase and costs more in operation. For short projects renting is usually more economical; for uses beyond two to three years buying pays off – not infrequently as a used, well-documented module. What matters is that transport, foundations, assembly and connections are in the calculation from the start, rather than surfacing only in the final invoice.
