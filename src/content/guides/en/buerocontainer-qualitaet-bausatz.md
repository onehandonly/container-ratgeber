## Why prices differ so much

Anyone comparing office containers will find offers that cost only a fraction for the same size. A new 20 ft office container from European manufacture costs roughly €8,000 to €12,000. Alongside it are new containers of the same size for considerably less – sometimes half.

Behind the cheap offers there is, in most cases, a different construction: the **flat-pack container**, also sold as a kit or folding container. It is manufactured in the Far East, disassembled and shipped flat-packed – usually **four containers in one bundle** taking up the space of a single shipping container. The dealer in Germany or another European country then assembles the containers themselves.

That saves transport costs, factory assembly time and material. And that is exactly where, in material and workmanship, the differences arise.

## Two construction methods compared

| Feature | Flat-pack container (mostly Far East) | Room container from European manufacture |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered as | disassembled, four units per bundle | fully assembled and fitted out |
| Assembly | by dealer or customer on site, bolted | in the factory, frame welded or factory-bolted |
| Frame | often thinner-walled profiles, frequently only painted | sturdy profiles, mostly hot-dip galvanised |
| Walls | sandwich panels, often with an EPS core | sandwich panels or stud construction, often with mineral wool |
| Insulation thickness | often less | designed for the central European climate |
| Joints | many panel joints, dependent on assembly quality | sealed in the factory |
| Electrics | sometimes pre-installed, compliance unclear | installed and tested to European standards |
| Structure, snow load | not always verified for German load assumptions | type structural calculation, often stackable over several storeys |
| Documentation | often incomplete, often not in German | verification of structure, fire safety, thermal insulation |
| Resale, hire | low residual value | established, high residual value |
| Price new, 20 ft (rough) | considerably lower | approx. €8,000–12,000 |

The table describes typical differences, not a rule without exceptions. There are well-built kits and weak European products. What matters is checking the following points on the specific offer.

## Where the differences lie in detail

### Frame and corrosion protection

The steel frame of floor frame, roof frame and corner posts carries the whole container. In kits the profiles are often thinner-walled and only painted. Where paint is damaged in transit or during assembly, rust begins. European room containers mostly have hot-dip galvanised frames that stay protected even at scratches.

### Insulation and fire safety

Many flat-pack containers use wall panels with an **EPS (expanded polystyrene)** core. EPS insulates reasonably but is combustible, melts in a fire and produces a lot of smoke. Panels with **mineral wool** are non-combustible. For a habitable room where people work, that is a significant difference – and it can become a problem for the permit or the insurer. Background in [Fire safety for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften).

Then there is the insulation thickness. Thin panels are fine for a tool shed, but as a winter office they mean high heating costs and cold wall surfaces.

### Joints and tightness

A kit consists of many individual parts that are bolted and sealed on site. Every joint is a possible path for rainwater, draughts and heat loss. How tight the container ends up depends heavily on how carefully it was assembled. Typical weak points are the roof-to-wall connections and the corners.

### Structure and snow load

In Germany a building must withstand regional snow and wind loads, which vary considerably with region and altitude. Room containers factory-built in Europe usually have a type structural calculation for this and are often stackable for two or three storeys. With kits, such verification is often missing or does not refer to German load assumptions. Many are intended for one storey only, or two at most.

### Electrics

Some kits come with pre-installed electrics. Whether they comply with the standards applicable here is not always clear – from sockets and cable cross-sections to circuit protection. Before use, a qualified electrician should inspect the installation and issue a test certificate. For commercial use an inspection is mandatory anyway.

### Documents for the building authority and insurer

If an office container stays permanently, it usually needs planning permission. For that the building authority requires verification of structure, fire safety and thermal insulation. European manufacturers generally supply these documents. For kits they are often missing or unusable, and having a planner produce them later can eat up much of the saving. More in [Building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung) and [The GEG and containers](/en/ratgeber/container-geg-energierecht).

## With shipping and storage containers, origin hardly matters

It is quite different with **shipping containers** and the **storage containers** derived from them. Most of them are built in the Far East, and have been for decades. There are practically no quality differences by origin, for a simple reason: shipping containers are manufactured to the international standard ISO 1496 and must pass tests for approval under the CSC convention. Production is supervised by inspection bodies, and every container carries an approval plate.

Whether a shipping container is good or bad is therefore decided not by the factory but by its **age and history** – the years in sea service, damage and care. How to assess condition is covered in [New, one-way or used?](/en/ratgeber/container-neu-oder-gebraucht) and [Reading the container number and CSC plate](/en/ratgeber/container-nummer-csc-plakette).

One exception you should know: **storage containers are also sold as kits** for bolting together. These are not shipping containers but separate designs. They have no CSC approval, are usually not stackable and are not meant to be transported loaded. For simple storage they may be enough – but they are not comparable with a shipping container.

## How to recognise a flat-pack container

- **Visible bolted joints** on the corner posts and along the wall panels.
- **Panel joints** at regular intervals, roughly every one to one and a half metres.
- **Delivery as a bundle,** or a note in the offer that the container is assembled on site.
- **Thin outer sheets** that can be dented with a thumb.
- **Bright white foam core** at cut-outs or cable entries – an indication of EPS.
- **Missing or foreign-language documents** on structure, electrics and insulation.

## Questions to ask the dealer

1. Who is the manufacturer, and where was the container made?
2. Is it delivered assembled or put together on site – and by whom?
3. What insulation is in the walls and roof, how thick, which fire classification?
4. Is the frame galvanised or only painted?
5. Is there a type structural calculation stating the permitted snow load, and is the container stackable?
6. Is there a test certificate for the electrics?
7. Which documents do I receive for the building application?
8. What warranty is there, and who is responsible for leaks?

A reputable dealer answers these questions without hesitation. If the answers stay vague, you know where you stand.

## When a kit can still be the right choice

| Use | Kit suitable? |
|---|---|
| Tool shed, garden store, simple shelter | yes, often entirely adequate |
| Site accommodation for one season | possible, with careful assembly |
| Clubhouse or kiosk with occasional use | possible, check fire safety |
| Permanent office workplace | rather not |
| Living, accommodation, nursery, school | no |
| Multi-storey or permit-requiring complex | no, hardly approvable without verification |
| Hire or later resale | rather not, low residual value |

## Do the whole calculation

The lower purchase price is only part of the cost. An honest calculation also includes assembly, higher heating costs from thinner insulation when used as an office, possibly subsequent verification for the permit, an electrical inspection and the lower resale value. For short-term use or as a store, the calculation often works out for the kit. For a permanently used workplace, the European-built room container is frequently the cheaper choice over the years. The basics of total cost are in [Running costs and depreciation](/en/ratgeber/container-betriebskosten-wertverlust).

## Important note

> This description reflects **typical differences** between construction methods and is not an assessment of individual manufacturers or dealers. Quality depends in each case on manufacturer, model and assembly. Prices are **rough orientation values without guarantee**. The documents and verification of the specific product are authoritative.

## Conclusion

With office containers, construction decides quality: flat-pack containers from the Far East are considerably cheaper but often fall short of factory-built room containers from European production in frame, insulation, fire safety, tightness and documentation. For storage and short-term use that may be enough, for permanent workplaces usually not. With shipping and storage containers, by contrast, standard and approval ensure uniform quality – there it is age and condition that count, not the country of origin.
