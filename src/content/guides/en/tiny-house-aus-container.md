A tiny house built from a shipping container uses an industrially produced, very rugged steel box as its shell. The appeal lies in the clear geometry, the short build time and the reuse of an existing component. Even so, it is no long-weekend DIY project: success or frustration depends mainly on things that have little to do with the container itself – the plot, the permit, the insulation and the ancillary costs. This article is the entry point to the subject; more detailed guides on costs, floor plans and law are linked where relevant. Legal references describe the situation in Germany.

## What speaks for and against a container tiny house

| Argument | What is true about it | What needs qualifying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | The load-bearing shell exists; most of the structural work and the roof are not needed. | Insulation, windows, bathroom, services and utility connections cost as much as in other small houses. Part of the saving simply comes from the smaller floor area. |
| Build time | Fit-out is largely weather-independent, with suppliers sometimes working indoors. | Permits and utility connections often take longer than the fit-out. |
| Mobility | A container is designed to be handled by crane and can be relocated. | A fitted-out, connected house is not moved casually. Large wall cut-outs weaken the shell; before any later transport, a structural engineer should assess it. |
| Sustainability | A used container is reused rather than recycled. | In operation, insulation and heating energy dominate the environmental balance. A poorly insulated container is not a sustainable house. |
| Flexibility | Well suited as a garden building with living quality, a granny annexe or a studio. | Whether an additional building is permitted on the plot is decided by planning law, not by the construction type. |

## Which containers are suitable

**20 feet** offers internal dimensions of approx. 5.90 × 2.35 m, i.e. around 13.9 m² of floor before fit-out. That is the smallest sensible module for a one-person tiny house, a garden office or a guest room. **40 feet** comes to approx. 12.03 × 2.35 m internally, around 28.3 m², and allows a genuine separation of living, sleeping and bathroom. All dimensions in detail are in [Container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse).

**High cube rather than standard.** A standard container is approx. 2.39 m high inside, a high cube approx. 2.70 m – around 30 cm more. How much of that remains after fit-out is shown in the calculation in the next section. For residential use, the high cube is generally the right choice.

**One-trip or used?** A one-trip container has carried a single cargo and has hardly any dents or rust. For a residential project the premium is usually money well spent, because less rust removal and recoating are needed. With an older used unit, ask about its history and inspect it on site; the [inspection checklist](/en/rechner/besichtigung-checkliste) helps. Regardless of age, the roughly 28 mm plywood floor of shipping containers is as a rule treated with wood preservatives at the factory. For residential use it is therefore usually removed or permanently and tightly covered. More in the guide [Living in a container](/en/ratgeber/wohncontainer-ausbauen).

**Combining several containers** is the usual route to larger floor plans. If two modules are placed side by side and the walls between them opened up, the removed wall sections must be structurally replaced. A container's load-bearing structure is the steel frame of corner posts and longitudinal rails, but the corrugated walls stiffen it – large cut-outs are therefore engineering work. Staggered layouts, L-shapes or a stacked second module noticeably increase planning and waterproofing effort. How such combinations are stacked and joined is covered in [Stacking and connecting containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln).

## Worked example: how much area and height remain after fit-out

A container's internal dimensions are not its living dimensions. Every insulated wall, the ceiling and the floor build-up cost centimetres. The figures below are example calculations with simplified assumptions, not a basis for design.

**Wall assumptions:** internal insulation on all four walls; a "slim" variant with approx. 75 mm total build-up per wall (roughly 60 mm PIR plus 15 mm lining) and a "thick" variant with approx. 115 mm (roughly 100 mm mineral wool plus 15 mm lining). The insulating values of these build-ups are compared in [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen).

| Module | Raw internal area | after 75 mm per wall | after 115 mm per wall | Area loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 ft | approx. 13.9 m² (5.90 × 2.35 m) | approx. 12.7 m² (5.75 × 2.20 m) | approx. 12.0 m² (5.67 × 2.12 m) | approx. 8.8–13.3 % |
| 40 ft | approx. 28.3 m² (12.03 × 2.35 m) | approx. 26.1 m² (11.88 × 2.20 m) | approx. 25.0 m² (11.80 × 2.12 m) | approx. 7.6–11.5 % |

The 20-foot container loses proportionally more, because the end walls weigh more heavily relative to its area. Partitions, bathroom and built-in units have not yet been deducted.

**Height assumptions:** ceiling and floor together take approx. 18 cm in the slim variant and approx. 30 cm in the thick one (ceiling insulation with lining plus a floor build-up of insulation, decking board and finish).

| Internal height before fit-out | clear height with 18 cm build-up | clear height with 30 cm build-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard, approx. 2.39 m | approx. 2.21 m | approx. 2.09 m |
| High cube, approx. 2.70 m | approx. 2.52 m | approx. 2.40 m |

This is the real reason for the high cube: after fit-out, a standard container ends up at around 2.10 to 2.20 m. For habitable rooms, many German state building codes (Landesbauordnungen) specify a clear room height of around 2.40 m, in some cases with lower values or exemptions for certain buildings. The rules of the relevant state apply; clarify the requirement before settling on container and build-up. If you want to keep the full area, insulate externally – often the better solution in building-physics terms anyway, although the typical corrugated look then disappears behind cladding.

## Sizes at a glance

| Combination | Raw internal area approx. | after internal insulation, roughly | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 ft standard | 13.9 m² | 12–13 m² | Garden office, studio, storage with workspace |
| 20 ft high cube | 13.9 m² | 12–13 m² | One-person tiny house, guest room |
| 40 ft high cube | 28.3 m² | 25–26 m² | Tiny house for 1–2 people |
| 2× 20 ft high cube | 27.7 m² | 24–26 m² | Two zones, e.g. living and sleeping/bathroom |
| 2× 40 ft high cube | 56.5 m² | 49–52 m² | Couple or small family |
| 3× 40 ft high cube | 84.8 m² | 73–78 m² | Family home, possibly on two storeys |

For multiple modules, the "after internal insulation" value is a rough estimate using the area loss from the worked example. Where walls between modules are opened up, no insulation is needed there; instead there are steel sections, sealing of the joints and possibly columns. Concrete room layouts are shown in [Floor plans for container tiny houses](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-grundrisse).

Proven layout logic in a 40-foot module: bathroom and services at one end wall to keep pipe runs short; kitchen along one long wall; living area facing the glazing; sleeping at the other end. Large openings belong in the long wall where possible, but need a steel frame there – details in [Retrofitting windows and doors](/en/ratgeber/container-fenster-tueren-nachruesten).

## Which fit-out level suits you?

| Criterion | Self-build | Weatherproof module + own interior fit-out | Turnkey |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Own labour | very high; several hundred hours is realistic | medium: floors, finishes, furniture | low |
| Specialist trades needed | electrics, gas if any, connections, structural engineering | electrics, connections | via the supplier |
| Building-physics risk | yours | shared | the supplier's, as far as the contract covers it |
| Cost control | depends heavily on your discipline | good, shell at a fixed price | good, if the scope is clearly described |
| Suits you if … | you are experienced with tools and have time | you want to hand over the critical trades but still save | time is short or financing requires firm quotes |

Whatever the level: electrical installations and gas systems belong in professional hands. With turnkey offers, check carefully whether foundation, transport, crane and utility connections are included – they often are not. Whether a ready-made container house is the better choice than your own build is discussed in [Container house: build or buy](/en/ratgeber/containerhaus-bauen-oder-kaufen).

## What a container tiny house roughly costs

The detailed calculations with every line item are in [What does a container tiny house cost?](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten), the trades in detail in [Fit-out costs by trade](/en/ratgeber/container-ausbaukosten-gewerke). For a first orientation, these orders of magnitude are enough:

| Block | Range approx. | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 40 ft container, used | €2,500–4,500 | standard-height values; high cubes are usually somewhat higher |
| 40 ft container, as new / one-trip | €4,500–7,000 | also standard height |
| Self-build 20 ft, total | €21,000–40,000 | incl. container, transport, foundation, planning application; excl. utility connections |
| Turnkey, total | €40,000–90,000 | incl. foundation; 20 ft towards the lower end, 40 ft towards the upper |
| Utility connections water/wastewater/power | €3,000–15,000 | biggest uncertainty |
| Planning, structural calculations, permit | €1,500–6,000 | already included in both totals above |
| Foundation | €600–8,000 | pad foundations to slab, see [Container foundations](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament) |

**Budgeting transport correctly.** Locally, a 20-foot container can be delivered by a lorry with a loader crane for approx. €350–800 per trip. A 40-foot container usually comes by articulated lorry (approx. €400–900 per trip locally); as the lorry cannot unload itself, a mobile crane at approx. €600–1,800 per day is added. Delivering a 40-foot module locally therefore comes to approx. €1,000–2,700, correspondingly more over longer distances. The figures come from [Transporting a container: delivery costs](/en/ratgeber/container-transport-lieferkosten); an estimate for your route is provided by the [transport cost calculator](/en/rechner/transportkosten).

On top come outdoor works such as a terrace and path, plus a contingency of about 10–20 % for the unexpected. Running costs – heating, insurance, property tax, corrosion protection – are broken down in the cost guide.

## The route to a tiny house in seven steps

1. **Define the use.** Permanent home, holiday unit or workspace? Permit, insulation standard and sanitary solution depend on it.
2. **Secure a plot.** The hardest step. Without a serviced site that is suitable under planning law, the best concept is worthless.
3. **Clarify the permit – before buying.** A preliminary talk with the building authority prevents expensive mistakes, including on the required room height.
4. **Choose and buy the container.** Fix size, height, condition and delivery point. Check that the lorry and, where needed, a mobile crane can reach the site. Purchase routes are compared in the [Buying a container](/en/kaufen-mieten/kauf) section.
5. **Choose the fit-out level.** See the decision table above.
6. **Foundation and connections.** Pad, strip or screw foundations, or a slab; in parallel water, wastewater and power.
7. **Fit-out, acceptance, move-in.** The [delivery acceptance report](/en/ratgeber/container-anlieferung-abnahmeprotokoll) helps on delivery day.

## Permits and plot

Under German building law, a container tiny house is a building as soon as it is used for people to stay in – regardless of whether it could theoretically be moved. Whether and under which procedure a permit is needed depends on location, duration and use. The relevant state building code (Landesbauordnung) and the local development plan apply, and in undesignated outlying areas additionally § 35 of the Federal Building Code (BauGB). Three questions come first: is residential use permitted on the plot under planning law? Will the house be a permanent residence or used only temporarily? Does the construction meet the requirements for fire safety, room height and thermal performance under the Buildings Energy Act (GEG)?

Campsites and weekend-house areas are generally not intended for permanent living. Procedures and documents are explained in [Containers and planning permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung), the specifics of finding a plot in [Tiny house: plot and law](/en/ratgeber/tiny-house-stellplatz-und-recht).

## Insulation, heating and moisture

Steel conducts heat very well – exactly what a building envelope should not do. Every metal connection running from inside to outside becomes a thermal bridge and thus a potential condensation point. The insulation layer must therefore either wrap continuously around the outside of the steel or, inside, be applied over the full surface with a properly sealed vapour control layer and no continuous metal sections.

Proven options are external insulation with a ventilated cavity and cladding, and internal insulation from rigid foam boards or spray foam (see [Spray foam insulation](/en/ratgeber/spruehschaum-daemmung-container)), in each case with airtight junctions. Mineral wool directly against the cold steel wall without a working vapour control layer easily leads to condensation and mould. Because a tiny house has little air volume but a lot of moisture from showering and cooking, it needs a well thought-out ventilation concept; mechanical ventilation with heat recovery is a very sensible solution. Suitable heating includes infrared panels, split air-conditioning units that also cool in summer, or small heat pumps. How heating load depends on the insulation standard is shown in [Containers in winter](/en/ratgeber/container-im-winter). If you plan without grid connections, see [Off-grid living in a container](/en/ratgeber/autarkes-wohnen-im-container).

## Common mistakes

- **Container bought first, permit clarified afterwards.** The most common and most expensive mistake.
- **Standard height instead of high cube.** After fit-out, around 30 cm are missing that cannot be recovered – possibly exactly the centimetres needed for the required room height.
- **Too many openings in the long walls.** Every larger opening needs structural replacement; at some point the steelwork costs more than the container.
- **Insulation treated as a minor item.** It determines comfort, running costs and service life.
- **No plan against summer overheating.** Allow for shading, roof overhang and light-coloured surfaces.
- **Access forgotten.** A 40-foot module needs a load-bearing access route and space for an articulated lorry and mobile crane.
- **Quotes compared without ancillary costs.** Foundation, transport, crane and utility connections are missing from many lump-sum prices.

If you want to gain experience first, start with a single 20-foot high cube as a garden office or studio – but local building law applies to that too.

## Important note

This article gives a general overview and does not replace advice from the building authority, an architect, a structural engineer or an energy consultant. Requirements for room height, thermal performance and fire safety differ by German state and individual case. All costs are guide values without guarantee.

## Conclusion

A container tiny house is not a bargain-basement option, but it is easy to budget. The shell is there, the build time is short, and the geometry forces clear decisions. Success is decided at four points: a plot that is sound under planning law, sufficient room height – in practice, a high cube –, insulation that is properly designed in building-physics terms, and a budget that includes foundation, transport, crane and utility connections from day one. At a realistic approx. €21,000–40,000 for a self-built 20-foot module or approx. €40,000–90,000 turnkey, each plus utility connections, it stays well below the cost of a conventional house – with correspondingly less floor area.
