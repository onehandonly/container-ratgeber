Container cabins look convincing in photos: plenty of glass, a timber deck, a view of the countryside. But anyone who wants to **rent one out** is running more than a weekend house. Different planning-law standards apply, levies and tax questions come into play, and guests have higher expectations than you would have of a garden house. This guide puts the decisions in the order in which they actually arise: first location and law, then fit-out, then the calculation.

> This guide describes the **German legal position in general terms** and is not legal or tax advice. Planning law is state law and is applied locally by the building authority and the municipality; details differ from state to state and municipality to municipality.

## Three knock-out questions before any planning

Before ordering a container, three questions should be answered. If any of them gets "no" or "I don't know", clarify it with the municipality first.

1. **Is holiday letting allowed at this location at all?** This is a planning-law question about the permitted use, not about how the building looks.
2. **Is the plot serviced?** Access, power, fresh water and a permissible wastewater solution are not options for guests but prerequisites.
3. **Will it operate all year or seasonally?** Insulation, heating and frost protection of the pipes depend on this – and with them a large share of the costs.

## Planning law: holiday letting is its own use

From the point of view of planning law, holiday letting is neither permanent residence nor a plain garden house. The location is therefore the decisive factor. The following table gives **rough orientation**, not an assurance for an individual case.

| Location | Assessment | What matters |
|---|---|---|
| Designated holiday or weekend-house area (special zone in the development plan) | Generally the intended place for holiday letting | Provisions of the development plan on size, form, roof shape and number |
| Campsite | Only within the site's permit | The operator decides what may stand on the site; state regulations on camping and weekend sites may set limits |
| Residential area (pure or general) | Assessed differently depending on zone type and scale | Development plan, scale of use, consideration for neighbours |
| Mixed-use area | Often more conceivable than in residential areas | Agreement with the municipality, possibly change of use |
| Outer zone (open countryside) | Generally not approvable | Holiday accommodation does not normally count among privileged projects there |
| Own garden at the main house | Depends on size, use and zone type | Letting an outbuilding remains a question of use |

The outer zone is the most common disappointment: a plot "in the countryside" is cheap precisely because there is no planning right for holiday accommodation there. The basics are in [Development Plan and Outer Zone](/en/ratgeber/container-bebauungsplan-aussenbereich), and the route to permission in [Building Application and Change of Use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung). Whether and in what form a container needs permission at all depends on location, duration and use – see [Does a Container Need Planning Permission?](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

The sequence matters: ask the municipality **before** buying the plot and the container, ideally in writing and with a preliminary enquiry. That is cheaper than any demolition order.

## What guests expect – and what 13 m² can give

A 20-foot high cube measures about 5.90 × 2.35 m inside with a ceiling height of about 2.70 m, so just under 14 m² of floor area. With internal insulation a few centimetres of width and height are lost, and realistically about 11–12 m² remain usable. That is enough for a double bed, a small bathroom and a kitchenette – but it is tight. A 40-foot container (about 12 m inside) offers almost twice the area and allows a real separation of sleeping and living.

| Area | Guest expectation | Planning note |
|---|---|---|
| Sleeping | Own bed from about 1.40 m width, storage | Bed across the end wall, storage under the bed |
| Bathroom | Shower, WC and basin, hot water | The wet room is the most expensive square metre; plan ventilation |
| Kitchen | Hob, fridge, sink, crockery | A run along one wall is enough for short stays |
| Comfort | Heating and – where sensible – cooling | Think of winter and summer separately |
| Outdoor space | Terrace, seating area, screening | This is where the impression of space is created |
| Quiet | No noise transmission, no condensation corners | Steel transmits sound; see [Noise Control and Acoustics](/en/ratgeber/container-laermschutz-akustik) |

A container fit-out for continuous operation must also solve **moisture and thermal bridges** properly, because holiday accommodation often stands empty and cools down between guests. Background and solutions are in [Thermal Bridges in Containers](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container) and [Insulating a Container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen). For the fit-out steps overall: [Fitting Out a Living Container](/en/ratgeber/wohncontainer-ausbauen). A [high cube container](/en/container/high-cube-container) suits as a starting model because of its ceiling height.

## Connections, safety and liability

Guests are not owners. What a resident would repair themselves becomes a rating on the booking platform. In addition, as operator you have a **duty to maintain safety** (Verkehrssicherungspflicht).

- **Power:** professional installation, residual-current protection and inspection by a qualified electrician; details in [Electrical Planning for the Interior](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau) and [Power Connection for Containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).
- **Water and wastewater:** a permissible disposal route must be clarified before permission; see [Water Connection and Wastewater](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser). For seasonal operation, pipes need frost protection and a thorough flush after periods of vacancy.
- **Fire safety:** smoke alarms in sleeping areas, a second escape route (for example a window of suitable size) and suitable extinguishing equipment; basics in [Fire Safety in Containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften).
- **Energy:** when fitting out a heated accommodation, requirements of German building energy law may apply; see [Energy Law and the GEG](/en/ratgeber/container-geg-energierecht).
- **Insurance:** a private buildings or contents policy does not automatically cover commercial letting. Clarify buildings, contents and liability cover explicitly with the insurer; see [Insuring a Container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern).

## Levies, taxes and registration

Letting brings obligations that do not exist for private use. Which ones exactly depends on the individual case and on your municipality:

- **Accommodation levy** (visitor's tax, tourism levy): regulated locally, with very different rates and registration duties.
- **Income tax:** income from letting is taxable. Whether it counts as income from letting and leasing or as commercial income depends, among other things, on the scope of services.
- **VAT:** a reduced rate can apply to short-term accommodation, and small businesses can, under certain conditions, use the small-business scheme. The thresholds change from time to time – please check the current position.
- **Trade registration:** from a certain scale of operation it may be required. Ask the trade office.
- **Misuse of housing:** in some cities and regions with a tight housing market there are rules that restrict the use of residential space as holiday accommodation or make it subject to permission.
- **Registration duties:** depending on state and guest group, registration obligations may apply; the rules have been adjusted several times in recent years.

On depreciation and the tax basics of containers see [Tax and Depreciation](/en/ratgeber/container-steuer-abschreibung). This does not replace tax advice.

## Worked example: does it pay off?

The following calculation is a **model with assumed values**, not a forecast. Above all, it shows how strongly the result depends on occupancy.

**Assumptions:**

- Investment for container, fit-out, furniture, terrace and connections: **€45,000** (within the range of the sample calculations in [Cost of a Container Tiny House](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten) plus furnishing, terrace and servicing)
- Price per night: **€95**
- Average stay: **2.5 nights**
- Cleaning and linen: **€45 per stay**
- Booking platform: **15 % of revenue**
- Fixed annual costs (power, water, insurance, levies, maintenance): **€2,400**

| Occupancy | Nights per year | Revenue | Surplus before tax | Payback of investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 % | approx. 91 | approx. €8,700 | approx. €3,300 | approx. 13–14 years |
| 35 % | approx. 128 | approx. €12,100 | approx. €5,600 | approx. 8 years |
| 50 % | approx. 182 | approx. €17,300 | approx. €9,100 | approx. 5 years |

Not included are your own time for guest communication and checks, financing costs, taxes, reserves for replacing furniture and equipment, and winter vacancy with uninsulated or unheated operation. The results are therefore more of an upper limit than an expected value. Anyone who calculates with 25 % occupancy and is still satisfied has the most robust calculation.

## Typical mistakes

- **Buying the plot first and clarifying the use later.** The most expensive mistake, because the location can no longer be changed.
- **Extrapolating occupancy from high-season weeks.** Annual occupancy and a midsummer weekend are two different figures.
- **Dimensioning too tightly.** A rainy night in 11 m² quickly gets cramped with two people; outdoor space and storage are not extras.
- **Fitting out for summer only.** If you want guests in winter, insulation, heating, ventilation and frost protection must be planned from the start.
- **Leaving out insurance and tax.** Both belong in the calculation before the first booking arrives.
- **Not involving the neighbours.** Complaints about noise or parking traffic are the most common reason a municipality looks more closely; see [Neighbour Law and Boundary Distance](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand).

## Checklist before you start

- Written information from the municipality on the permitted use (preliminary enquiry)
- Access, power, water and wastewater solution clarified and costed
- Decision made on year-round or seasonal operation
- Fire safety (smoke alarms, second escape route) planned
- Insurance cover for letting confirmed in writing
- Levies, tax and registration duties clarified with the municipality and tax adviser
- Calculation with realistic occupancy, including the 25 % scenario

## Important note

This guide serves general orientation and is **not legal or tax advice**. Planning law is state law (state building codes) and is applied locally; tax, registration and levy rules change. All figures are indicative values without guarantee. Binding information is available from the building authority, municipality, tax office or qualified advisers.

## Conclusion

A container as holiday accommodation can work if three things are right: a location where holiday letting is permitted, a fit-out that survives guest expectations and the seasons, and a calculation that still holds at low occupancy. The container itself is the smallest part of the problem; law, servicing and operation decide. Start with the conversation at the municipality, not with buying the container.
