A steel container can survive decades outdoors, but only with regular inspection. Rust is not a sudden event; it is a creeping process that you can stop early with just a few simple steps. This guide explains how rust forms, how to care for your container, how to treat existing rust properly, and what maintenance intervals you should realistically plan for.

## How and why rust forms

Most shipping and storage containers are made of Corten steel, a weather-resistant structural steel. This material forms a firmly adhering surface layer of rust that protects the steel underneath and slows further corrosion. Corten steel is therefore designed to rust superficially, but it is not rust-proof. Shipping containers are also painted; the paint is the first line of protection, and the steel itself only comes into play where it is damaged. Under permanent moisture, standing water or damaged paintwork, corrosion can penetrate deeper and lead to rust-through.

Ordinary steel, sometimes used on repair patches or cheap add-on parts, lacks this self-protection and rusts through much faster. When making additions or carrying out welding work, always use suitable material.

The main causes of rust are:

- **Condensation inside:** The most common trigger. Warm, humid air condenses on cold steel walls ("container rain") and keeps the interior permanently damp.
- **Scratches and chips:** Damaged coating exposes bare steel. Corrosion starts there in spots and works its way under the paint layer.
- **Standing water:** Puddles on the roof, blocked drainage openings in the frame and water in the base frame attack the steel from the outside.
- **Salt and aggressive substances:** Coastal locations, road salt or chemical stored goods accelerate corrosion considerably.

Anyone who knows these factors can counteract them specifically before holes develop.

## Regular care

The best rust prevention is a recurring visual inspection. Take time twice a year for a thorough check, ideally in spring and autumn.

**Cleaning:** Remove leaves, dirt and bird droppings from the roof and frame. These deposits hold moisture and promote corrosion. Cleaning with clear water and a soft brush is usually enough. Avoid aggressive pressure washing from a short distance, as this can damage the coating.

**Check and maintain seals:** The rubber door seals keep water out. Check them for cracks, hardening and leaks. Clean the seals and treat them with silicone or rubber care so they stay supple. Also lightly grease the hinges and locking bars.

**Keep drainage openings clear:** Depending on the design, the frame has small openings that carry off condensation and rainwater (the floor itself has no drain holes). If existing openings are blocked, water builds up in the profile and the frame rusts from the inside. Keep these openings consistently clear.

**Check the roof:** The roof is the most heavily loaded surface. Look for puddles, dents and rust spots. Standing water should be able to drain off; small dents can often be avoided by setting up the container with a slight slope.

## Treating rust – step by step

If you discover a rust spot, you should act promptly. Smaller spots can easily be treated yourself.

1. **Remove rust:** Mechanically remove loose rust with a wire brush, sandpaper or grinder down to sound metal. The surface must be dry and free of grease and dust. For heavy corrosion, a rust converter can also help.
2. **Prime / rust protection:** Apply a rust-protection primer, for example zinc- or synthetic-resin-based. The primer isolates the steel from oxygen and moisture and is the decisive layer against renewed corrosion.
3. **Paint:** Once the primer has dried, apply a weatherproof top coat, ideally in two thin layers. It protects the primer mechanically and against UV radiation. Use compatible products from a single coating system.

For large-area rust-through, affected load-bearing corners or holes in the floor, a proper welding repair is required. Here you should call in a specialist, as structural safety is affected. For the floor see [Repairing or replacing a container floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen).

## Prevention

Prevention is cheaper than repair. A few measures keep moisture permanently in check.

**Ventilation against condensation:** The biggest problem arises inside. Ensure air exchange through vents, roof ventilators or occasional opening of the doors. Dehumidifiers or desiccants provide additional help, especially for moisture-sensitive stored goods. Interior insulation reduces condensation but must be built correctly in terms of building physics (see [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen) and [Thermal bridges](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container)). More on ventilation: [Ventilating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung).

**Location and ground:** Do not place the container directly on soil or grass. A dry, load-bearing base of point foundations, concrete slabs or paving keeps the base frame free of waterlogging and allows air to circulate underneath. Details in the guide [Container foundations](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament). A slight slope lets rainwater run off the roof.

**Roof coating:** An additional roof or full coating protects the most heavily stressed surface and extends service life. Reflective coatings also lower the interior temperature and thus condensation.

## Maintenance intervals at a glance

The table below summarises the most important tasks and their recommended intervals. The figures are approximate values and depend on location, climate and use.

| Maintenance task | Recommended interval |
| --- | --- |
| Visual check for rust and dents | every 6 months |
| Cleaning of roof and frame | every 6–12 months |
| Check and maintain seals | annually |
| Grease hinges and locks | annually |
| Check/clean drainage openings in the frame | annually, in autumn |
| Treat small rust spots | as needed, immediately |
| Refresh top coat / roof coating | every 3–5 years |
| Thorough specialist inspection | every 5 years |

> Practical tip: Anyone who consistently carries out the six-monthly visual check prevents almost all costly rust-through damage, because incipient rust can be stopped with little effort.

## How bad is it? Rust grades and actions

| Finding | Recognisable by | Action | Urgency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surface rust | Reddish haze, paint intact or barely damaged | Clean, sand lightly, touch up paint | at the next maintenance |
| Paint damage with rust | Open spot, rust under the paint, blistering | Remove rust, prime, paint twice (see above) | promptly, before the next rainy period |
| Widespread rust / pitting | Clear material loss, rough surface | Remove rust over a large area, renew the coating system | planned within a few months |
| Rust-through, or rust at corners and frame | Holes, swollen edges, soft steel when tapped | Specialist firm, welding repair, structural assessment if needed | immediately; do not lift or stack the container |

For load-bearing parts (frame, corner posts, corner castings) the result of the inspection decides on lifting, transport and stacking. More in [Lifting a container correctly](/en/ratgeber/container-richtig-anheben) and [Stacking and coupling containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln). For the floor see [Repairing or replacing a container floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen).

## Worked example: paint requirement for a 20 ft container

The outer surface (without floor) of a 20 ft container can be estimated from the external dimensions of about 6.06 × 2.44 × 2.59 m:

- Roof: 6.06 × 2.44 ≈ 14.8 m²
- Two long sides: 2 × 6.06 × 2.59 ≈ 31.4 m²
- Two end faces: 2 × 2.44 × 2.59 ≈ 12.6 m²
- Total ≈ 58.8 m²

The corrugated profile enlarges the area actually to be painted compared with this flat calculation; an allowance of roughly 20–30% is a reasonable rough assumption, i.e. about 70–76 m². With an assumed coverage of 6–10 m² per litre per coat and two top coats, that gives roughly 14–25 litres. You will also need primer for rust spots and abrasives. The coverage stated in the product's technical data sheet is always what counts; surface roughness and application change consumption considerably.

## Checklist for the six-monthly inspection

- Roof: puddles, dents, rust spots, leaves in the corrugations
- Frame and corners: paint damage, blisters, swollen edges
- Lower edges: contact with soil, grass or waterlogging
- Doors: seals, hinges, locking bars, whether they close properly
- Interior: condensation marks, mould spots, rust specks on walls and ceiling
- Floor: soft or swollen areas, discolouration at the edges
- Ventilation: grilles and roof vents clear and intact

## Lifespan and expectation

A well-maintained steel container easily reaches 20 to 30 years or more in stationary use. What matters is not the age but the condition of the frame, floor and coating. Neglected containers with standing water and open rust spots, on the other hand, can rust through after just a few years. The effort for care is small compared to the value it preserves.

If you are considering a purchase anyway, it pays to look closely at condition and prior damage before buying. You can read what to watch out for in the guide [Buying a used container](/en/ratgeber/gebrauchten-container-kaufen).

## Important note

The information is general guidance. The processing and safety instructions of the manufacturers of paints, primers and rust converters take precedence; respiratory and eye protection are required when sanding or blasting, and old coatings may contain harmful substances. For damage to load-bearing parts this guide is no substitute for a professional assessment.

## Conclusion

Rust on a container is not fate but a question of care. Corten steel does protect itself superficially, but condensation, scratches and standing water can lead to rust-through. With six-monthly checks, clear drainage openings, well-maintained seals and good ventilation, you keep moisture in check. Treat small rust spots immediately and refresh the paint regularly. This keeps your container functional and valuable for decades.
