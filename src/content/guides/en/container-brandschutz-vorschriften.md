## The common misconception

"A steel container can't burn anyway." That sentence comes up in almost every conversation about container fire safety – and it misleads, because it answers the wrong question.

First, the steel does not burn, but everything that goes inside does: insulation, lining, flooring, furniture, electrical installation, stored goods. The container is only the shell. Second, steel loses load-bearing capacity quickly under fire exposure – unprotected structural steel begins to yield substantially somewhere above roughly 500 °C, and that temperature is reached within minutes in a fully developed fire. Unprotected steel therefore achieves no meaningful fire resistance duration on its own – all the more so for the comparatively thin-walled sections of a container. Where a fire resistance class is required, the structure needs a lining (such as fire protection boards) or a tested coating system.

Third – and this is the core of building law – fire safety is not primarily about the structure but about people. The protection objectives in the German state building codes are always the same in substance: prevent the outbreak and spread of fire and smoke, enable the **rescue of people and animals**, and permit **effective firefighting**.

Those three objectives determine what applies to your container.

## The decisive threshold: habitable room or not

The largest jump in requirements sits between a pure storage container and a container where people stay for more than a passing moment.

| Use | Fire safety level | Typical requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Storage container, no occupancy | low | Distance to buildings, no fire load on escape routes, compliant electrics |
| Site container, habitable room | medium | Second exit or window, smoke alarms, extinguisher, escape route signage |
| Office container, permanent | medium to high | Escape routes, distances, component requirements by size and storeys |
| Residential container | high | Two independent escape routes, smoke alarms, requirements for structure and separating walls |
| Multi-storey container installation | high | Fire safety concept, separation between units, ladder access where required |
| Assembly or retail use | high to very high | above the special-building thresholds of the state building code, special building rules and a concept by a specialist |

A **habitable room** in building law is a room intended for people to stay in for more than a passing period. As soon as a container is that – office, break room, living space, teaching – requirements for escape routes, daylight and ceiling height apply. It is the same threshold as for permits; see [Building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung).

## Escape routes: the most important requirement in practice

For habitable rooms the state building codes require, in principle, **two independent escape routes**. The first normally runs through the necessary corridor or directly to the outside. The second can be a further door, an external staircase or – where reachable – a window accessible by fire brigade ladder.

For containers this has very concrete consequences:

- **A single exit is often not enough.** The classic office container with one door at the end meets the second escape route requirement only if a suitable window exists.
- **Rescue windows must be usable.** The German model building code (Musterbauordnung) requires windows serving as an escape route to have a clear opening of at least 0.90 m × 1.20 m and a sill no higher than 1.20 m above the floor; the state codes largely adopt this, and the exact values are set out in the respective state code. A narrow tilt window is not an escape route. The worked example below shows what this means inside a container.
- **With stacked containers** the second escape route from the upper floor becomes the core problem. Either a second external staircase, or a ladder-accessible window with a standing area for the fire brigade. That standing area needs load capacity and access – a meadow behind the building does not work.
- **Escape routes must stay clear.** The most common defect on construction sites is simply material in front of the door.
- **Doors along escape routes** should open in the direction of escape and be openable from the inside without a key. A container locked from the outside with people inside is a serious defect. The original double doors of a shipping container with locking bars are unsuitable for this; for habitable use an additional personnel door is usually installed.

## Worked example: does a rescue window fit into the container?

The minimum dimensions sound unproblematic, but in a container they run into the ceiling height. A standard container measures approx. 2.39 m inside, a high cube approx. 2.70 m (see [Sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse)).

| Step | Standard (approx. 2.39 m inside) | High cube (approx. 2.70 m inside) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sill at the permitted maximum | 1.20 m | 1.20 m |
| plus clear window height | + 1.20 m | + 1.20 m |
| Top of the clear opening | 2.40 m – higher than the interior | 2.40 m – approx. 0.30 m to spare |
| Sill lowered to 0.90 m | top at 2.10 m, approx. 0.29 m left for frame, lintel and top side rail | top at 2.10 m, approx. 0.60 m reserve |

Three practical points follow:

1. **In a standard container the sill must sit well below 1.20 m**, because the window frame, lintel and top side rail take up additional height. Anyone retrofitting a window should fix the clear dimensions (not the outer frame dimensions) in advance.
2. **The opening weakens the wall.** A cut-out roughly 1 m wide in the corrugated wall needs a surrounding steel frame; how this is done is described in [Retrofitting windows and doors](/en/ratgeber/container-fenster-tueren-nachruesten).
3. **On the upper floor, height above ground also counts.** A standard container is approx. 2.59 m high externally. If the floor of the upper container is at approx. 2.59 m and the sill at 0.90 m, the top of the sill is approx. 3.50 m above ground; with three stacked standard containers, the top floor sill is at approx. 6.10 m. Under the model building code, a second escape route via the fire brigade's portable ladders is only envisaged up to a sill height of 8 m above ground; above that, an aerial ladder of the local fire brigade or a structural second escape route is needed. Whether the fire brigade accepts a ladder-based escape route at all is decided case by case by the fire safety department – for stacking itself see [Stacking and coupling containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln).

## Distances between containers and to buildings

To stop a fire spreading, the building codes require distances or, alternatively, fire walls. The specific dimensions are set by state law. The system of the German model building code serves as orientation: if an external wall stands closer than 2.50 m to the plot boundary, it must in principle be built as a fire wall (for smaller buildings, depending on the building class, a wall with lower fire resistance may suffice) – unless a distance of at least 5 m to existing or future permissible buildings is secured. This gives the following magnitudes:

| Constellation | Rough orientation | Alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Container to neighbouring building | 5 m between buildings as orientation from the system above | Fire wall or component with corresponding fire resistance |
| Container to container, separate units | Distance or fire-rated separation | Separating wall with fire resistance, often F30/F90 required |
| Row of containers as one unit of use | permissible as a continuous block | limited total area, observe the concept |
| Container to plot boundary | under 2.50 m, requirements for the external wall; separation distances per state building code in addition | see [Neighbour law](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand) |
| Container next to gas store, filling station, transformer | heightened requirements | case-by-case assessment mandatory |

Fire resistance classes are usually described as **F30** (fire retardant, 30 minutes) and **F90** (fire resistant, 90 minutes); the newer European designation is REI 30 or REI 90. Which class is required depends on building class, number of storeys and use – a single single-storey office container is assessed differently from a three-storey modular residential block.

## Insulation and fit-out: the actual fire load

Because the steel does not burn, the interior fit-out determines fire behaviour. Building materials are classified under DIN 4102 from A1 (non-combustible) to B3 (highly flammable), and under the European DIN EN 13501-1 from A1 to F.

| Insulation material | Usual classification | Fire-relevant property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mineral wool (rock, glass) | non-combustible (A1/A2) | first choice where fire safety governs |
| Calcium silicate, foam glass | non-combustible | expensive but unproblematic |
| EPS / polystyrene | flame retardant (B1) to normally flammable | melts and drips, heavy smoke development |
| PUR / PIR | B1 to B2 depending on product | good insulation values, strong smoke gases in a fire |
| PU spray foam | product-dependent, usually B2 without added protection | requires a thermal barrier towards the room |
| Wood fibre, cellulose | normally flammable (B2) | usable with a covering, verification needed |

This is one of the strongest reasons to choose mineral wool for habitable rooms, or to cover combustible insulation on the room side with a non-combustible lining such as plasterboard or gypsum fibreboard. For spray foam this thermal barrier is explicitly mandated in the US and is technically indispensable here too; details in [Spray foam insulation](/en/ratgeber/spruehschaum-daemmung-container).

The second major factor is **smoke development**. The large majority of fire deaths are caused by smoke gases, not flames. In the small air volume of a container, heavy smoke makes a room impassable within seconds – another reason why the second escape route weighs so heavily here.

## Smoke alarms, extinguishers, electrics

**Smoke alarms** are now mandatory in dwellings in all German states – at least in bedrooms, children's rooms and corridors serving as escape routes; some states require them in all habitable rooms. Installation and maintenance duties differ by state. This applies to residential containers exactly as to a masonry house. In commercially used containers, requirements follow from occupational safety law and the risk assessment; see [Occupational safety on site](/en/ratgeber/container-arbeitsschutz-baustelle).

**Fire extinguishers** belong in commercially used containers. In German workplaces, provision is sized under the technical rule ASR A2.2 using **extinguishing agent units (Löschmitteleinheiten, LE)**. For basic provision at normal fire risk: up to 50 m² floor area 6 LE, up to 100 m² 9 LE, up to 200 m² 12 LE. How many LE an extinguisher provides follows from its rating stated on the label; the conversion is set out in the ASR.

| Example | Floor area (approx. inside) | Basic provision under ASR A2.2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One 20 ft office container | approx. 13.9 m² (5.90 × 2.35 m) | 6 LE |
| Three coupled 20 ft containers | approx. 41.6 m² | 6 LE |
| Six coupled 20 ft containers | approx. 83.2 m² | 9 LE |

If the risk assessment identifies an elevated fire risk – such as a workshop with flammable liquids – additional measures apply. In offices and habitable rooms a foam extinguisher is often preferred, because a powder extinguisher in the small volume of a container wipes out visibility instantly and heavily soils electronics and furnishings; in front of server racks CO₂ extinguishers are common (see [Server and technical room](/en/ratgeber/container-als-serverraum-technikraum)). Extinguishers are usually inspected every two years by a competent person.

**Electrical installation** is a typical cause of container fires. Improvised wiring, overloaded extension leads and damaged cables running under the container door are classics. For commercial use, insist on the prescribed inspection of fixed electrical installations, and have cable penetrations through steel walls fitted with proper grommets – a sharp sheet edge will chafe through insulation over months.

How circuits, protection and residual current devices are best planned in a fit-out is covered in [Electrical planning for the fit-out](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau).

**Heating** is the second classic. Electric fan heaters and radiators close to combustible material are a frequent cause of fire in site containers. Gas heaters inside containers are fundamentally problematic because of carbon monoxide and explosion risk, and unacceptable without proper flue routing and ventilation.

**Batteries and hazardous substances** fundamentally change the assessment. Charging lithium batteries, keeping gas cylinders or storing flammable liquids in a container creates an additional ignition source or fire load to which separate rules apply – see [Storing batteries and hazardous substances](/en/ratgeber/container-akkus-gefahrstoffe-lagern).

## Checklist before putting a container into use

The following overview summarises what should at least be clarified for each use. It does not replace review by the authority or a fire safety planner, but helps avoid overlooking anything fundamental.

| Check | Storage | Office/break room | Living | Stacked/multi-storey |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distance to buildings and boundary clarified | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Second escape route (door or rescue window) | – | yes | yes | yes, per storey |
| Personnel door openable from inside without a key | – | yes | yes | yes |
| Insulation non-combustible or covered on the room side | if insulated | yes | yes | yes |
| Smoke alarms | – | per risk assessment | yes | yes |
| Extinguishers under ASR A2.2 | for commercial use | yes | recommended | yes |
| Electrical installation inspected, penetrations protected | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Heaters kept clear of combustibles | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Access and standing area for the fire brigade | – | if ladder rescue | if ladder rescue | yes |
| Fire safety concept | only with hazardous substances | for special buildings | for special buildings | regularly |

Special uses with many people face additional requirements; see [School rooms and interim nurseries](/en/ratgeber/container-schulraum-kita-interim), [Events and catering](/en/ratgeber/container-fuer-events-und-gastronomie) and [Snack bar and kiosk in permanent use](/en/ratgeber/container-imbiss-kiosk-dauerbetrieb).

## When a fire safety concept becomes necessary

A concept prepared by a qualified specialist is regularly required for:

- multi-storey container installations, particularly from three storeys upwards
- larger continuous units of use or exceeded floor areas
- special buildings: accommodation, day nurseries, schools, assembly venues, retail
- accommodation for large numbers of people, such as staff housing
- uses with elevated fire risk, such as storage of flammable liquids

In these cases the verification forms part of the building application. Costs for a concept range roughly from mid to upper four figures depending on scope – considerably less than retrofitting afterwards.

## Important note

> This article contains **general information** and constitutes neither **legal advice nor fire safety planning**. Fire safety requirements derive from the **building code of the respective German state**, from special building regulations, from occupational safety law and from assessment of the individual case. The dimensions, classes and costs mentioned are rough orientation values without guarantee. Binding requirements are set by the responsible building supervisory authority, the fire safety department and a qualified fire safety planner.

## Conclusion

The fact that steel does not burn is almost incidental for fire safety. What matters is the second escape route (which in a standard container needs careful planning because of the ceiling height alone), sufficient distance to neighbouring buildings, non-combustible or covered insulation, working smoke alarms and sound electrics. For a single storage container this stays manageable. As soon as people occupy it – and all the more with stacked or multi-storey installations – fire safety belongs in the design from the start, not in a retrofit.
