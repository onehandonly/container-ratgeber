A shipping container is, out of the box, nothing more than a weatherproof steel box – sturdy, stackable and cheap to buy. It only becomes suitable for living, working or overnight stays once it has been properly fitted out. And that is where the real work lies: not the container itself, but the insulation, building services and interior finishing decide whether you end up with a cosy room or a damp metal box that overheats in summer and cools out in winter. This guide walks through the key steps in practical terms.

## What container homes are good for

Container conversions are long past being just a stopgap for building sites. Typical uses include:

- **Tiny house:** A 20-foot container offers roughly 14 m² inside, a 40-foot container or two 20-foot units joined together roughly 28 m² of floor area (before deducting insulation and lining). A popular choice for permanent small-footprint living; floor plans are shown in [tiny house floor plans in a container](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-grundrisse).
- **Office & home office:** An insulated container in the garden creates a quiet workspace separate from the house.
- **Garden room & studio:** A retreat, hobby workshop or guest room without extending the main house.
- **Holiday home:** As seasonal accommodation, often with generous glazing towards a terrace.

For all of these the same rule applies: as soon as people spend time inside on a regular basis, the same building-physics requirements apply as for a house.

## Insulation: the be-all and end-all

Steel conducts heat more than a hundred times better than wood (roughly 50 versus 0.13 W/(m·K)). An uninsulated container wall is therefore one large **thermal bridge**: in winter heat escapes almost unimpeded, in summer the metal heats up strongly in the sun. The most serious problem, however, is **condensation**. When humid indoor air meets the cold inner steel surface, it drops below the dew point and water forms – the wall "sweats". The results are mould, corrosion and saturated insulation.

The solution lies in a well-thought-out combination of insulation, vapour barrier and ventilation. In principle there are two routes:

**Internal insulation** is the most common variant. A stud frame is fixed to the steel wall, the cavities filled with mineral wool or PU boards, and a **vapour barrier** applied on the room side before the lining goes on. Advantage: relatively simple to carry out. Disadvantage: it eats into valuable interior space and the dew point can move critically close to the steel wall. The vapour barrier must therefore be genuinely gap-free. Why internal insulation barely solves the thermal bridges at the steel frame is explained in [thermal bridges in containers](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container).

**External insulation** in the form of an external thermal insulation composite system (ETICS) or a ventilated facade places the insulant on the outside. This keeps the steel in the warm zone, sharply reduces the condensation risk and preserves the interior space. Disadvantage: more effort, and the distinctive container look disappears beneath the facade.

> Rule of thumb: if you want to keep the dew point safely out of the construction, insulate on the outside. If you want to keep the industrial look and build more cheaply, insulate inside – but then with a carefully executed vapour barrier and planned ventilation.

| Insulation option | Insulant (examples) | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal, stud frame | Mineral wool, wood fibre | cheap, DIY-friendly | costs interior space, vapour barrier critical |
| Internal, PU/PIR boards | PU/PIR rigid foam | slim, high performance | pricier, bonding demanding |
| Sprayed PU (in-situ foam) | Polyurethane spray foam | seamless, seals well | needs specialist, hard to remove (see [spray foam insulation](/en/ratgeber/spruehschaum-daemmung-container)) |
| External ETICS | EPS, mineral wool | dew point uncritical, full interior | pricier, hides container look |

Do not forget: the **floor and roof** must be insulated too, otherwise the thermal bridges simply move there. The original floor is treated plywood or hardwood and is often contaminated with wood preservatives; for habitable rooms it is therefore usually removed or sealed over. More in [replacing or restoring the container floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen). Insulation in detail is covered in [insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen).

### Worked example: how much space insulation costs

A 20-foot container measures roughly 5.90 × 2.35 m inside, about 13.9 m² of floor area. Assume internal insulation of 100 mm plus 12.5 mm lining, i.e. 112.5 mm per wall:

- Width: 2.35 m − 2 × 0.1125 m ≈ 2.13 m
- Length: 5.90 m − 2 × 0.1125 m ≈ 5.68 m
- Usable area: about 12.1 m² instead of 13.9 m² – roughly 13 % less.

The height shrinks too. With a standard container (2.39 m inside), about 2.20 m remain after a 112.5 mm roof build-up and around 80 mm of floor build-up. The German model building code (Musterbauordnung) specifies 2.40 m for habitable rooms; the state building codes partly differ. For permanent living the **High Cube** (2.70 m inside, about 2.51 m afterwards) is therefore often the better choice. Check the minimum height with the building authority beforehand.

## Building services: heating, power, water, ventilation

**Heating:** For small, well-insulated containers an electric solution often suffices. **Infrared heaters** are cheap to buy and quick to install, but power-hungry in continuous use. More efficient is an **air-conditioning split unit or air-source heat pump**, which additionally cools in summer – a double benefit for containers. Plain electric convectors are a fallback. For permanent living, efficiency matters, as the low thermal mass of the steel structure favours rapid temperature swings. Power supply is covered in [power connection for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).

**Electrics:** Installation belongs in the hands of a qualified electrician. You need a dedicated consumer unit with a residual-current device (RCD), enough sockets, lighting and – for a heat pump or instantaneous water heater – suitably sized supply cables. The steel body is earthed and included in equipotential bonding. Connection to the house circuit or a separate meter point must be clarified; details in [electrical planning in a container](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau).

**Water & plumbing:** A bathroom or kitchen requires supply and drainage lines. With a fixed installation you connect to the public utilities; frost-proof routing of pipes is essential. Connection options and disposal are described in [water supply and wastewater](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser). Where no sewer connection is possible, collection tanks, small treatment plants or composting/dry toilets are options.

**Ventilation:** The most important and most frequently underestimated point. A tightly insulated container is almost airtight – without controlled air exchange humidity builds up and mould threatens. Regular purge ventilation helps; more comfortable and safer is a **mechanical ventilation system with heat recovery**, which extracts stale, humid air without wasting the heat. Ventilation concepts are in [ventilating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung).

## Windows, doors & interior finishing

Windows and doors are created by **cutting into the steel wall**. This is quick with an angle grinder or plasma cutter, but weakens the structure: the corrugated side walls are load-bearing. Larger openings must therefore be **reinforced** with welded-in steel frames or profiles so the container retains its rigidity. This work belongs in expert hands and should be accounted for at the planning stage. Practical details: [retrofitting windows and doors](/en/ratgeber/container-fenster-tueren-nachruesten).

The actual **interior finishing** then proceeds like conventional drywall construction: plasterboard, OSB or timber panels go onto the stud frame, followed by paint, tiles or panelling. Electrical and water lines disappear into the wall cavities. Step by step, the raw steel box becomes a habitable room with even walls and ceiling.

## Permits & energy standard

This section describes the German legal position. A container converted for living is, in building-law terms, a building (structure) – and permanent residential use generally **requires planning permission**. The basis is the state building codes (Landesbauordnungen); in the outlying area (Außenbereich) section 35 of the Federal Building Code (BauGB) applies in addition. Whether permission is needed and in which procedure depends on location, duration and use. The local building supervisory authority is responsible; the development plan and type of use are decisive. Permanent residential use is regulated considerably more strictly than pure storage or building-site use. Our guide on [container building permits](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) covers the details; for a change of use see [building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung).

Depending on the case, the requirements of the German Buildings Energy Act (GEG) also apply: heated residential buildings must meet insulation and system standards unless an exemption applies (for example for buildings with a limited period of use). In that case ambitious insulation is not only a matter of comfort but a precondition for approval. The classification is explained in [GEG and containers](/en/ratgeber/container-geg-energierecht). An early line to the building authority saves a lot of rework; [fire protection](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften) and the [foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament) matter as well.

## Cost range of the fit-out

The container itself is usually the smaller item – the fit-out accounts for most of the cost. How much a used or new container costs is shown in our guide [what does a container cost?](/en/ratgeber/was-kostet-ein-container). The following table gives rough reference points for the trades involved in a 20-foot container (about 14 m²) as a habitable room without sanitary facilities, carried out by specialist firms. The values match the ranges in [fit-out costs by trade](/en/ratgeber/container-ausbaukosten-gewerke), where you will also find the individual items.

| Cost block | approx. range | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Windows & doors (2 windows, 1 door) incl. cut-outs | €3,000–7,000 | incl. welding in frames |
| Insulation and interior lining (walls, floor, roof) | €5,000–11,000 | depends on option; about 60–70 m² of surface |
| Electrics | €1,800–4,000 | qualified firm, with sub-board and earthing |
| Heating / AC split | €1,500–3,500 | infrared cheaper, heat pump pricier |
| Floor and surfaces | €1,700–3,700 | incl. removing the old floor |
| **Total without sanitary** | **approx. €13,000–29,200** | approx. €930–2,090 per m² of floor area |
| Sanitary (WC, basin) with connection | additional approx. €3,500–12,500 | heavily dependent on connection |

Not included are container, transport, foundation and ventilation equipment. To put the totals in context: the office example in the trade-by-trade calculations comes to roughly €22,900 including the container; with sanitary facilities the total rises to about €30,000 to €40,000. The running-cost side of living (heating, power, depreciation) is covered in [running costs and depreciation](/en/ratgeber/container-betriebskosten-wertverlust).

Doing a lot yourself can save money – but electrics, plumbing and load-bearing cut-outs should be left to specialist firms.

## Order of work (checklist)

1. Clarify permit question, minimum height and connections (power, water, sewer) with the building authority and utilities.
2. Choose the container: preferably a High Cube for living, checked for rust and floor condition ([inspection](/en/ratgeber/container-besichtigung-pruefprotokoll)).
3. Build the foundation, place the container.
4. Have openings cut and reinforced.
5. Remove or build over the old floor, create the floor build-up with insulation.
6. Insulation, vapour barrier and wall build-up, run cables and pipes.
7. Electrical and plumbing installation, sign-off by the specialist firm.
8. Lining, surfaces, commission heating and ventilation.
9. Only then move in, and during the first heating season monitor humidity (hygrometer) and condensation.

## Important note

> All figures are non-binding approximate values (as of 2026) and serve only for rough orientation; they do not replace legal or professional advice. Actual costs depend heavily on region, specification, own labour and connection situation; permit requirements depend on the state building code, location and use. Obtain specific quotes and clarify permit questions with the competent authority.

## Conclusion

A container can be fitted out into a fully-fledged living, office or holiday space – provided the building physics are right. The key is **insulation**: only insulation prevents heat loss, overheating and, above all, condensation on the cold steel walls. A vapour barrier, controlled ventilation and an efficient heat source such as an AC heat pump make the room comfortable all year round. Equally important: clarifying the building permit and energy requirements early on, since permanent residential use generally requires planning permission in Germany. Anyone who plans these points cleanly and leaves the critical trades to professionals ends up with a lastingly dry, warm and individual space – from a home office in the garden to their own tiny house.
