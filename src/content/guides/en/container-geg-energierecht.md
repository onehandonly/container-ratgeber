## What the GEG is about

The German Building Energy Act (Gebäudeenergiegesetz, GEG) merged the former Energy Saving Ordinance (EnEV), the Energy Saving Act and the Renewable Energies Heat Act in 2020. It sets requirements for the thermal protection of the building envelope and for building services, governs what share of heat must come from renewable sources, and determines when an energy performance certificate is required. This article describes the legal situation in Germany.

For containers this matters for a practical reason: an uninsulated steel container meets none of these requirements even remotely. If your project falls under the GEG, insulation is no longer a matter of comfort but part of the legal requirements – and in a container it costs noticeable interior space. If it does not, there is nothing to verify, but heating bills and the risk of condensation remain real.

## Three questions in the right order

Rather than starting with insulation thicknesses, it pays to check the classification in three steps. Each answer decides whether the next question still matters.

### Question 1: Is the container heated or cooled?

The GEG applies to buildings that, according to their intended purpose, are **heated or cooled** using energy. An unheated storage container, a bicycle shelter or a materials store without heating is simply not an energy-law issue. An electric frost protection heater in a technical room does not normally turn a store into a heated building within the meaning of the act; once people work or live there and the space is conditioned for that purpose, things are different.

Special case reefers: a refrigerated container operated as a transport or storage unit is a cooling machine rather than a building with space conditioning. Here, electricity costs and equipment are the main issues – see [Reefer containers: operation and electricity costs](/en/ratgeber/kuehlcontainer-betrieb-stromkosten).

### Question 2: Does an exemption apply?

The GEG excludes certain buildings from its scope altogether. For containers, these exemptions are the most relevant:

- **Buildings intended to be repeatedly erected and dismantled**, and **provisional buildings with a planned period of use of up to two years**. The classic case is the site container that moves from one construction site to the next.
- **Residential buildings intended for use for less than four months a year**, or whose expected energy consumption is below a quarter of that for year-round use – relevant, for example, to accommodation used only seasonally.
- **Operational buildings that, according to their purpose, are heated for less than four months** and cooled for less than two months a year.
- There are also exemptions for livestock buildings, greenhouses, tents and air-supported halls, which rarely concern containers.

What matters is the **intended purpose and planned period of use**, not the construction method. A container is not exempt merely because it could in theory be moved. If you plan an office container for five years at the same spot from the outset, you cannot rely on the two-year exemption. And if a provisional solution keeps being extended, have the classification reviewed – at the latest with a change of use or a new application, the authority will look more closely. Building law is closely linked; see [Building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung) and [Containers and planning permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

### Question 3: Is it a "small building"?

This is the question most often overlooked. The GEG defines **small buildings** as buildings with **no more than 50 m² of usable floor area**. Two relaxations matter for containers:

1. **Simplified verification:** if a newly erected small building meets the maximum thermal transmittance values (U-values) that the GEG sets for installing or replacing external building components, the new-build requirements are deemed to be met. A full balance of primary energy demand and transmission heat loss is then not needed.
2. **Room modules:** the same simplification applies to buildings intended for a period of use of **no more than five years** and made up of **room modules of up to 50 m²** usable area each – for example, a multi-module container facility planned as an interim solution.

In addition, the **energy performance certificate** provisions do not apply to small buildings.

How the usable area is determined matters. For non-residential buildings it is the net floor area; for residential buildings it is the usable area calculated under the GEG from the heated building volume, which can differ from the internal dimensions. The figures below are therefore internal floor areas for initial orientation only, not verification values:

| Configuration | Internal area approx. | Classification (guidance) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One 20 ft container (5.90 × 2.35 m) | approx. 14 m² | well below 50 m² |
| One 40 ft container (12.03 × 2.35 m) | approx. 28 m² | below 50 m² |
| Two coupled 40 ft containers | approx. 57 m² | above 50 m², full new-build requirements – unless the room-module rule applies (up to five years) |
| Three coupled 20 ft containers | approx. 42 m² | close to the limit; the GEG calculation decides |

Whether a specific facility stays within the limit should be calculated by an energy consultant – in borderline cases the calculation method is decisive.

## The classification at a glance

| Situation | GEG relevance (guidance) | Practical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unheated storage container | not covered | no energy verification |
| Site container moved from site to site | generally exempt | the manufacturer's standard insulation is legally sufficient |
| Rented office container, planned for up to two years | usually exempt, case by case | document the planned period on site |
| Single office or residential container, permanent | covered, but a small building | meeting the component U-values is sufficient; no energy certificate |
| Facility of modules up to 50 m² each, up to five years | covered, simplified route | as for a small building, U-values per component |
| Coupled facility above 50 m², permanent | covered | full new-build verification, energy certificate |
| Seasonal accommodation used less than four months a year | exempt if so intended | record the usage concept in writing |

## What is required – two routes

### The simplified route via U-values

For small buildings, every external component must meet a maximum U-value. The values below correspond to the maximum values the GEG sets for external components when they are installed or replaced (guidance; the current text of the act is authoritative). Insulation thickness is estimated as **d ≈ λ / U**, i.e. for the insulation layer only and without thermal bridges through the steel frame:

| Component | Maximum U approx. | Thickness at λ 0.035 | at λ 0.032 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| External wall | 0.24 W/(m²·K) | approx. 146 mm | approx. 133 mm |
| Flat roof (container roof) | 0.20 W/(m²·K) | approx. 175 mm | approx. 160 mm |
| Floor to outside air or ground | 0.30 W/(m²·K) | approx. 117 mm | approx. 107 mm |
| Windows | 1.3 W/(m²·K) | – | – |
| External door | 1.8 W/(m²·K) | – | – |

The formula is deliberately simplified. In practice, the continuous steel sections of the corner posts and longitudinal rails worsen the actual U-value unless they are covered by insulation. A designer will therefore often allow a little more insulation or place the insulation layer on the outside.

### The full verification

For buildings above 50 m² that do not fall under the room-module rule, the GEG uses two parameters: the **annual primary energy demand** and the **thermal protection of the envelope** (for residential buildings, the specific transmission heat loss). Both are assessed against a reference building of the same geometry. Here the overall balance counts: a slightly weaker wall can be partly offset by better building services. The GEG also requires a **permanently airtight envelope** and that thermal bridges be taken into account.

## Worked example: what internal insulation costs you

A standard 20 ft container has an internal floor area of approx. 5.90 × 2.35 m and a height of 2.39 m. Assume internal insulation of 140 mm plus approx. 30 mm for battens and lining, i.e. approx. 170 mm per side on the long walls and end walls:

| Dimension | Before | After internal insulation approx. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal width | 2.35 m | 2.01 m |
| Internal length | 5.90 m | 5.56 m |
| Floor area | approx. 13.9 m² | approx. 11.2 m² |
| Area lost | – | approx. 19 % |

Add ceiling insulation and an insulated floor build-up and the clear height drops as well – in a standard container with 2.39 m it quickly falls well below 2.20 m. A high cube with 2.70 m internal height leaves more margin. That is why **external insulation** is not only better in building-physics terms for a container, because it wraps the steel frame as a thermal bridge, but also saves space. Details in [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen) and [Thermal bridges in containers](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container); how the trades show up in the budget is covered in [Fit-out costs by trade](/en/ratgeber/container-ausbaukosten-gewerke).

## Heating: the renewable energy requirement

Since the 2024 GEG amendment, newly installed heating systems are generally required to run on at least **65 per cent renewable energy**. For new buildings in new development areas this applies directly; other cases have transitional periods linked to municipal heat planning. The heating rules are repeatedly up for political revision – check the current position before you plan.

| Heating system | Classification (guidance) | Note for containers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air-to-air or air-to-water heat pump | meets the requirement | a split air-conditioner with heating mode suits the small heating load well |
| Direct electric / infrared heating | only under conditions, including enhanced thermal protection | conceivable in a very well insulated small container; clarify the verification |
| Pellet stove / biomass | meets the requirement | storage space, flue and acceptance by the chimney sweep required |
| District heating | generally meets it | a connection is rarely available at a container site |
| Gas heating without renewable share | does not meet the requirement | not a sensible option for a new building |

Because of the small floor area, the heating load of a well-insulated container is low. Small heat pumps therefore become an option and the investment stays manageable. If you want to generate your own electricity, see [Solar panels on a container](/en/ratgeber/photovoltaik-auf-dem-container).

## Energy performance certificate

An energy performance certificate is generally required when a building is newly erected, sold or let. It is not needed for buildings outside the GEG – the unheated store and the site container – and, as described above, for **small buildings up to 50 m²**. A single residential or office container therefore normally needs no certificate, even if it stands permanently and has to meet the U-values.

For larger facilities in permanent use, a **demand-based certificate** derived from the calculation must be issued for a new building. It is usually produced as part of the verification that is needed anyway.

## Who prepares the verification and what it costs

The verification is usually prepared by an energy consultant, a building physicist or the architectural practice handling the design. Costs depend heavily on the route – the ranges below are rough guide figures without guarantee:

| Route | Effort | Cost approx. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small building: verify component U-values | assess build-ups, check manufacturer data | approx. €300–1,000 |
| Full new-build verification with energy certificate | balance, thermal bridge assessment if needed | approx. €1,500–4,000 |

Reputable manufacturers of residential and office containers often supply component data on which a verification can be based; with flat-pack kits it is frequently missing, as described in [Office containers: quality and kits](/en/ratgeber/buerocontainer-qualitaet-bausatz).

## Checklist before you buy

- [ ] Will the container be heated or cooled – and for what use?
- [ ] How long is it meant to stand on site? Record the planned period of use in writing.
- [ ] Will it be moved and re-erected regularly?
- [ ] What is the usable area – single unit, or coupled above 50 m²?
- [ ] What U-values do wall, roof, floor, windows and door have according to the manufacturer?
- [ ] Internal or external insulation, and what width and height remain?
- [ ] Which heating system is planned, and does it meet the renewable energy requirement?
- [ ] Is an energy certificate required, or is it waived as a small building?
- [ ] Who prepares the verification, and is it submitted with the building application?

## Funding

There are funding programmes for energy measures, chiefly the federal funding for efficient buildings (BEG – heating replacement, individual measures) and loan programmes for efficient new buildings. As a rule, the application must be made **before the measure begins** and qualified professional support must be involved. Whether a container is eligible depends on whether it counts as a building under the programme concerned – more likely for permanent modular construction than for a mobile container. Clarify this in writing with the funding body or an energy consultant beforehand.

## Important note

> This article contains **general information** and is **not legal, tax or energy advice**. It describes the situation under German law. The GEG is amended regularly; scope, exemptions, maximum values, heating rules and funding conditions may change at short notice and are interpreted by the competent authorities. All U-values, insulation thicknesses, areas and costs are guide figures without guarantee. Whether planning permission is needed depends on location, duration and use. Binding statements can only be made by the competent building authority and a qualified energy consultant.

## Conclusion

With a container, the first decision is not the insulation thickness but the classification: unheated stores, site containers that are repeatedly moved and provisional buildings of up to two years stay outside the GEG. A single office or residential container in permanent use is covered, but is usually a small building of up to 50 m² – then the U-values per component are sufficient and no energy certificate is needed. Only coupled facilities above 50 m² require the full new-build verification. Clarify this before you buy and plan the insulation on the outside where possible, and you avoid both unnecessary reports and an interior only two metres wide.
