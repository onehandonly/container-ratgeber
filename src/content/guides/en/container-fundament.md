## Why the Ground Determines Service Life

A container looks robust enough to simply set down anywhere. In practice, though, the ground beneath it is the most frequently underestimated factor. A shipping container weighs roughly 2,200 kg (20 ft) to about 4,000 kg (40 ft high cube) when empty – see [container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse). It transfers its entire load, which in use is far more than the empty weight, through its four lower corner castings – in other words, through very small bearing areas. If the ground beneath is not load-bearing or not level, problems arise that only become visible after months.

Four points are decisive:

- **Settlement:** Soft, uncompacted or freshly filled soil yields under point loads. If individual corners sink unevenly, the steel frame distorts. Doors then jam, no longer close, or stay permanently misaligned.
- **Standing moisture:** If the container sits directly on soil or in a hollow, water collects beneath the floor. Constant moisture attacks the underside and accelerates corrosion.
- **Ventilation from below:** A small gap between the container floor and the ground allows air to circulate. It keeps the underside dry and prevents condensation from lingering.
- **Rust:** Direct ground contact is the main enemy. Where steel rests permanently damp and without airflow, it rusts through from below – often unnoticed, because the spot cannot be inspected.

The basic rule is therefore: the container should stand **level, on load-bearing ground, and with a little clearance from the soil**. How elaborate the foundation needs to be depends on use, duration of placement and ground conditions.

## The Common Foundation Types at a Glance

### Compacted Gravel with Paving Slabs

The simplest load-bearing solution: the topsoil is removed, a layer of gravel is laid in, compacted, and finished with concrete paving slabs (typically 50 × 50 cm) under the four corners. The slabs distribute the point load and create a defined, level bearing surface.

**Advantages:** inexpensive, feasible as DIY, fully reversible, no permanent structure. **Disadvantages:** insufficient on very soft ground or slopes, levelling requires care.

### Concrete Pad Footings

A concrete footing is cast under each corner (and additionally at the centre for long containers), usually founded below the frost line – in Germany often around **80 cm deep**; the required frost depth depends on the site (regionally up to about 1 m), and the local figure comes from the building authority or a structural engineer. The pads transfer the load directly into load-bearing ground.

**Advantages:** high load capacity, frost-safe, durable, material-efficient compared with a full slab. **Disadvantages:** precise positioning required, excavation and concrete needed, levelling the individual pads is demanding.

### Strip Foundation

Two continuous concrete strips run lengthwise beneath the two longitudinal floor beams of the container. This distributes the load linearly and works well where the ground is load-bearing only in places.

**Advantages:** good load distribution, stable, suitable for heavy or coupled containers. **Disadvantages:** higher concrete and excavation effort than pad footings, a professional contractor is usually advisable.

### Concrete Slab

A continuous, reinforced concrete slab as a full-area foundation. The standard for permanent installations, especially for residential and office containers with high demands on levelness and water-tightness.

**Advantages:** highest load capacity and longevity, perfectly level, ideal when connecting water/wastewater, a good base for insulation. **Disadvantages:** the most expensive option, relevant for planning and permits, practically irreversible, requires a professional contractor.

### Screw Piles and Ground Anchors

Galvanised steel screws or ground anchors are driven into the soil and carry the container via adjustable supports. A comparatively recent, dry construction method without concrete.

**Advantages:** no excavation and no curing time, immediately load-bearing, easy to level, reversible, low environmental impact. **Disadvantages:** a soil report is advisable, difficult on rocky ground, special equipment or a contractor needed.

## Making the Right Choice: Use, Duration and Soil

Three questions lead to the right type of foundation.

**Use:** A simple storage container places lower demands on levelness than a residential container, in which doors and windows should close to the millimetre. For residential and office containers, pad footings, a strip foundation or a slab are the realistic options.

**Duration of placement:** For temporary use of a few weeks or months, the gravel-and-slab solution is almost always sufficient – it can be removed without a trace. The more permanent the location, the more a frost-safe concrete or screw foundation pays off. Important: a fixed foundation can influence the building-law assessment – whether a container needs a permit depends on location, duration and use – see the [container building permit guide](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and [zoning plans and outlying areas](/en/ratgeber/container-bebauungsplan-aussenbereich) (German rules).

**Ground conditions:** Load-bearing, natural soil allows simple solutions. Soft, clayey, filled or alternately wet soil calls for a deeper, frost-safe foundation – this is where pad footings and screw piles show their strengths. When in doubt, a ground investigation is worthwhile, especially before an expensive slab.

## Worked Example: Load under the Corners

For orientation, an example with deliberately simple assumptions (not a structural calculation): a 20 ft storage container weighing about 2,300 kg empty is loaded with 10,000 kg of cargo, evenly distributed.

- Total mass: 2,300 kg + 10,000 kg = 12,300 kg, equal to a weight force of about 121 kN.
- Per corner: about 30 kN (assuming even distribution and level support; uneven loading or a corner that does not bear firmly shifts this).
- Under a 50 × 50 cm paving slab (0.25 m²) this is a pressure of about 121 kN/m² on the gravel layer beneath.

Whether the ground can carry this depends on soil type, compaction and water table: compacted natural gravel behaves very differently from clayey or filled ground. If in doubt, have the ground assessed. For comparison, at the maximum gross mass of a 20 ft container (empty weight plus about 28 t payload, roughly 30 t together) the load per corner rises to about two and a half times this; when stacking, the containers above add to it – see [stacking and coupling containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln).

A second example for the material needed for the simple gravel bed: for a 20 ft container (about 6.06 × 2.44 m externally, around 14.8 m²) a bed of roughly 7 × 3 m is excavated. At a 25 cm layer thickness that is 7 × 3 × 0.25 = 5.25 m³ of compacted gravel, plus the slabs under the corners. This volume and the disposal of the spoil are the real cost drivers of this option, not the four slabs.

## Levelling and Bearing Points

Decisive for stability are the **four lower corner castings**, the nodes of the steel frame. The load should be transferred through them. The supports therefore belong exactly beneath the corners; for containers from around 12 m in length, additional bearing points at the centre are recommended to prevent the floor frame from sagging.

Levelling should be carried out with a water level hose or a rotating laser across all supports. A slight tolerance of a few millimetres is uncritical; more important is that **all four corners bear firmly**. If the container rests on only three points, the frame twists. To compensate for small height differences, galvanised steel plates or shim plates are suitable – not wood, which rots.

## Drainage, Slope and Ventilation

A good foundation accounts for water. The surrounding ground should fall slightly **away from the container** (a guide value of around 1–2 %) so that rainwater runs off and no puddle forms beneath it. The container itself stands level on its supports. A layer of gravel or crushed stone under the container acts as drainage and carries moisture away.

The deliberate **clearance from the soil** – through slabs, footings or supports – serves two purposes: it keeps the underside dry and enables the air circulation that dries out condensation. For permanent installations, it is also advisable to keep the area free of vegetation so that ventilation is maintained and moisture is not retained.

## DIY or Professional Contractor?

The gravel-and-slab solution and simple levelling can be carried out well as DIY with some manual skill and tools (plate compactor, laser, gravel) – this is the classic DIY case. As soon as **concrete, frost-safe founding, reinforcement or utility connections** come into play, however, the arguments favour a professional contractor: structural design, correct founding depth and lasting levelness are decisive here and error-prone. Screw piles require special equipment anyway. For residential and office containers, as well as on difficult ground, the professional contractor is the safe choice.

## Common Foundation Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topsoil (humus) not removed | Settlement, container tilts | Remove humus, compact gravel |
| Only three corners bear | Frame twists, doors jam | Bed all four corners firmly, shim with steel plates |
| Timber blocks as permanent supports | They rot, the container sinks | Galvanised steel or concrete parts |
| Hollow without water outflow | Standing moisture under the floor, rust | Ground falling away, gravel as drainage |
| Utilities not considered | Later cables require breaking out | Lay conduits for power and water in advance, frost-safe routing |
| Foundation without thinking about removal | Costly demolition when relocating | Clarify the period of use beforehand |

If you connect power, water or wastewater, plan the routes together with the foundation – see [power connection](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss) and [water supply and wastewater](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser). For residential use the fit-out follows: [converting a container into a home](/en/ratgeber/wohncontainer-ausbauen). Rust protection for the underside is covered in [care and rust protection](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz).

## Important Notice

> This article gives general orientation and replaces neither a ground investigation nor a structural calculation. Bearing capacity, frost depth and permit requirements depend on the individual case and, in Germany, on the federal state (state building codes; in outlying areas also section 35 of the Federal Building Code, BauGB). For permanent sites, residential use, stacking and difficult ground, consult a structural engineer or specialist contractor and the competent building authority.

## Conclusion

The right foundation is not a matter of principle but of weighing up. For the temporary storage container on firm ground, compacted gravel and paving slabs are sufficient. For the permanent residential container, there is hardly any way around pad footings or a slab. In every case, three things are decisive: a load-bearing, frost-safe foundation, a clean bearing of all four corner castings, and ground that drains water and allows ventilation. Anyone who plans carefully here prevents settlement, jamming doors and rust from below – and considerably extends the container's service life. When in doubt, a soil report and a concrete quotation from a professional contractor are worth more than any rule of thumb.
