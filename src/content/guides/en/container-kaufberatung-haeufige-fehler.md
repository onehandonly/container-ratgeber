At first glance, buying a container looks simple: pick a size, compare prices, have it delivered. In practice, the most expensive problems rarely come from the purchase price itself – they come from questions that got too little attention before ordering. This article collects the mistakes that keep coming up in practice, and shows how to avoid them.

## Mistake 1: Judging condition from photos alone

Photos reveal little about corrosion-through, floor damage or leaking seals – especially in backlight or from an unfavourable angle, a defect can look harmless. Anyone who buys a container unseen carries the full risk for defects that only surface on delivery and are then hard to negotiate.

**Better:** Insist on a viewing, or a detailed video with close-ups of the floor, corners and seals. A structured inspection sequence is covered in [Buying a used container](/en/ratgeber/gebrauchten-container-kaufen); a record to tick off is in [Inspection and test record](/en/ratgeber/container-besichtigung-pruefprotokoll), and the [inspection checklist tool](/en/rechner/besichtigung-checkliste) helps you prepare. The differences between new and used stock are explained in [New or used?](/en/ratgeber/container-neu-oder-gebraucht). Also check the container number and CSC plate: [Container number and CSC plate](/en/ratgeber/container-nummer-csc-plakette).

## Mistake 2: Ignoring the ground and structural loads

Not every surface can carry a loaded or converted container long-term. If the container is placed directly on grass or loose gravel, it settles unevenly – doors jam and stress cracks appear at the corner castings. Living or office conversions add further weight through insulation, screed and furniture.

**Better:** Plan the foundation before delivery, not after. Which solution is enough depends on use and ground: a simple storage container often manages with point foundations or a load-bearing gravel bed with slabs under the corner areas; living or office use, coupled containers or soft ground make strip foundations or a slab more realistic. According to the foundation guide, costs range from about €150 (gravel with slabs) to about €8,000 and more (concrete slab). Details in [Foundations for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).

## Mistake 3: Checking planning law only after delivery

A common and costly mistake: the container is already on the plot when it turns out it needs a permit or breaches the local development plan. In that case the authority may, depending on the situation, order the use to stop, the container to be removed, or impose a fine – whether it was bought or rented. Whether a permit is needed depends on the federal state, location, duration and use; the relevant rules are the state building codes (Landesbauordnungen) and, outside built-up areas, section 35 of the Federal Building Code (BauGB).

**Better:** Clarify permit requirements, boundary distances and permitted use before you order. The basics are covered in [Container planning permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and [Development plans & land outside built-up areas](/en/ratgeber/container-bebauungsplan-aussenbereich).

## Mistake 4: Underestimating access and delivery

A container cannot simply be carried. If there is no access for a hook-lift truck or crane, delivery becomes expensive, gets delayed, or fails on the day. Narrow driveways, low gateways or sloped ground are the most common obstacles.

**Better:** Measure passage width, headroom and manoeuvring space before booking a delivery date, and discuss the required equipment with the supplier. More in [Transporting a container](/en/ratgeber/container-transportieren) and [Transport & delivery costs](/en/ratgeber/container-transport-lieferkosten).

## Mistake 5: Underestimating insulation and thermal bridges for living/office use

A steel container is uninsulated by design, and its steel walls are vapour-tight. Converting it into living or office space without a proper insulation concept leads to cold bridges in winter and overheating in summer – often combined with condensation and mould risk on the steel profiles.

**Better:** Plan insulation and thermal-bridge treatment from the start, not as a later fix. See [Thermal bridges in containers](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container) and [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen); for living use, energy-efficiency requirements also apply ([GEG](/en/ratgeber/container-geg-energierecht)).

## Mistake 6: A purchase contract without documented condition

Verbal assurances about condition ("it's basically almost new") are worthless in a dispute. Without a written condition report, photos and clear warranty terms, buyers often have no recourse when defects appear later – especially with private sellers, where excluding warranty is permitted and common. If a private individual buys from a dealer, consumer rights apply instead: for used goods the period can be shortened under narrow conditions, but warranty cannot be excluded. Between businesses, an exclusion is possible.

**Better:** Get condition, year of manufacture and agreed defects confirmed in writing, and clarify beforehand whether and to what extent warranty applies. Details in [Purchase contract & warranty](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufvertrag-gewaehrleistung).

## Mistake 7: Forgetting insurance

Containers are often treated as "accessories" and end up in no insurance policy at all – until storm damage, break-in or vandalism reveals that neither home contents nor building insurance applies. Free-standing containers on building sites or used as storage are a popular target for theft.

**Better:** Check before buying which insurance fits your intended use. Overview in [Insuring a container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern).

## Mistake 8: Looking only at the purchase price

The price in the quote is rarely what you end up paying. Delivery, crane, base and accessories come on top. An example using the guideline figures from [What does a container cost?](/en/ratgeber/was-kostet-ein-container) (used 20-foot storage container):

| Item | Amount (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Container | €2,400 |
| Delivery by crane truck | €600 |
| Base and accessories (assumption) | €300 |
| **Total budget** | **€3,300** |

The total budget is therefore about €900, or a good third, above the purchase price. Anyone planning with €2,400 only faces a funding gap before the container is even on site. Depending on the project, foundation, insurance and maintenance come on top. Whether buying is the right choice at all is covered in [Buy or rent?](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufen-oder-mieten) and the [buy-or-rent calculator](/en/rechner/kaufen-oder-mieten); running costs are compared in [Running costs and depreciation](/en/ratgeber/container-betriebskosten-wertverlust).

## Mistake 9: Paying in advance to unknown sellers

A suspiciously cheap offer, pressure to transfer money quickly and no storage location you can visit are classic warning signs of fraud. Anyone who pays up front without knowing the seller may end up with neither goods nor money. What such schemes look like and how to check sellers is covered in [Fake container dealers and advance-payment fraud](/en/ratgeber/fake-containerhaendler-vorkasse-betrug). As a rule: check the imprint and commercial register entry, arrange a viewing, and pay only on handover or against an invoice with a clear scope of delivery.

## Mistake 10: Overlooking ownership and site consequences

If you place a container on someone else's land or with third-party financing, clarify who legally owns it and how it fits into the land register, lease or charge on the property. This matters above all for permanently installed containers; see [Containers and land: ownership, temporary fixtures, land register](/en/ratgeber/container-eigentum-scheinbestandteil-grundbuch) (German law). Theft protection also belongs in the plan: [Securing a container](/en/ratgeber/container-sichern-diebstahlschutz).

## Decision aid: warning sign and response

| Warning sign | Possible consequence | Response |
|---|---|---|
| No viewing possible | Hidden defects, fraud risk | Postpone or decline the purchase |
| No written condition report | No room to negotiate or claim | Get condition confirmed in writing |
| Foundation "will hold up somehow" | Settling, jamming doors, cracks | Assess load-bearing capacity in advance |
| Permit "not needed round here" | Removal order, fine | Ask the local building authority |
| Access "should be fine" | Failed or expensive delivery | Measure access precisely beforehand |
| Insulation "add it later" | Cold bridges, condensation, mould | Plan the insulation concept before conversion |
| Only the purchase price is budgeted | Funding gap from delivery and base | Calculate the total budget, here about +€900 |
| Advance payment to an unknown seller | Loss of the money | Check the seller, arrange a viewing, no advance payment |

## Pre-purchase checklist

- Condition inspected in person or by video (floor, corners, seals, roof)
- Ground and foundation thought through for the intended use
- Permit requirements and development plan clarified
- Access measured and agreed with the supplier
- For living/office use: an insulation concept in place
- Condition, year of manufacture and defects recorded in writing in the contract
- Suitable insurance clarified
- Total budget including delivery, base and accessories calculated
- Seller checked (imprint, viewing), no advance payment to unknowns

## Important note

> This article does not replace legal or structural advice for your specific case. Statements on permit requirements, structural loads and contract law are general and can vary by state, municipality and individual case. For larger projects, seek qualified advice (building authority, structural engineer, lawyer).

## Conclusion

The most expensive mistakes when buying a container almost always happen before delivery: unchecked condition, missing foundation, unresolved planning law, underestimated access, forgotten insulation, verbal instead of written agreements, missing insurance, a budget calculated too tightly, and unreliable sellers. Working through these points beforehand doesn't just make the purchase cheaper – it makes it far more predictable.

**Note on German law:** Several points above, especially planning permission and contract warranty, refer to German rules. If you are outside Germany, check the equivalent local building and consumer-protection rules.
