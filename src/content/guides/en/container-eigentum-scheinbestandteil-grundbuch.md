## Why ownership becomes a practical question

As long as a container stands on your own land and stays there, the question rarely arises. It becomes urgent when something happens: the plot is sold, a bank wants to register a land charge, a lease ends, the supplier asserts a retention of title, or a creditor steps in. In each case it must be clear **whether the container is a movable item or part of the land**.

This guide covers the civil-law side under the German Civil Code (BGB). It complements the articles on [building permits](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and [tax](/en/ratgeber/container-steuer-abschreibung), which apply their own, independent tests. It describes German law; individual cases may differ.

## The basic rule: essential component or temporary fixture

The BGB distinguishes two cases:

- **Essential component of the land** (sections 93 and 94 BGB): buildings and other things firmly attached to the ground legally belong to the plot. They cannot be sold or pledged separately but share the fate of the land. Anyone who builds them on someone else's land normally loses ownership to the landowner (section 946 BGB).
- **Temporary fixture** (section 95 BGB): a thing attached to the ground only for a **temporary purpose** does not become part of the land. The same applies to buildings erected by someone exercising a right over another person's land, for example a tenant farmer or lessee. The container remains a movable item and stays with whoever acquired it.

The deciding factor is not construction alone but the **intention at the time of attachment**, as far as it is recognisable to third parties. A hire agreement with an obligation to remove, a fixed-term lease or an expressly agreed time limit are strong indications of a temporary purpose. No court outcome can be predicted from this; it remains a case-by-case assessment.

## Which features point which way

The overview below lists typical indicators. No single row decides on its own.

| Feature | Points towards temporary fixture | Points towards essential component |
|---|---|---|
| Placement | loose on point foundations or supports, movable | set in concrete, welded, joined to a strip or slab foundation |
| Connections | pluggable, detachable (power, water via couplings) | fixed supply lines, drainage in the ground |
| Duration | limited, e.g. project or lease term | unlimited, intended permanently |
| Legal relationship | lease, hire or loan with duty to remove | ownership of the land with no intention to remove |
| Planning | removal provided for in contract or application | approved as a permanent building |
| Fit-out | reuse planned | bespoke fit-out that can hardly be removed |

A residential container on a strip foundation with a connected sewer, approved as a permanent building, will usually be part of the land. A storage container on four concrete slabs that a lessee must collect at the end of the contract usually will not. There is a lot of grey area in between – see [foundations](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament) for the technical side.

## Everyday consequences

### Sale of the plot

What is an essential component passes to the buyer with the land, even if the sale contract does not mention the container. A temporary fixture, by contrast, remains the property of the previous holder unless it is sold with the plot. When selling or buying land you should therefore deal with the container **expressly** in the contract: included or not, at what price, in what condition. Silence is the most common source of disputes.

### Land charges and financing

A land charge covers the plot together with its essential components (sections 1120 ff. BGB). A permanent container house can therefore raise the lending value, a merely placed temporary fixture usually does not. Banks typically require a building permit and a fixed connection for construction finance; more in [building or buying a container home](/en/ratgeber/containerhaus-bauen-oder-kaufen) and [tiny house costs](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten).

### Retention of title by the supplier

If the seller delivered the container under retention of title and it becomes part of the land through firm attachment, the retention may **lapse**. The seller is then limited to the payment claim. If you finance or buy in instalments, check whether the contract allocates this risk differently; see [purchase contract and warranty](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufvertrag-gewaehrleistung).

### Access by third parties

A temporary fixture can be seized by the owner's creditors like any movable item, whereas an essential component is realised together with the land. If you own a container on **someone else's** land, you should be able to prove ownership and location, for example with an invoice, photos and the lease.

## The land register: what is in it and what is not

The land register (Grundbuch) records **plots**, their owners and encumbrances (land charges, easements, third-party rights). A container does not appear there as an entry of its own. What matters in practice:

- **Land charges** burden the plot together with its essential components. Check before buying what the plot is liable for.
- **Easements** such as rights of way or for utility lines can restrict where you may place a unit. An easement in rem binds successors in title, a mere agreement between neighbours does not automatically; cf. [neighbour law and boundary distances](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand).
- **Heritable building right (Erbbaurecht):** here the building is an essential component of the building right and may belong to the holder. The building right has its own land register sheet.
- **Building encumbrances (Baulasten)** are not in the land register in Germany but in the building encumbrance register of the building authority, where state law provides for one. Anyone buying a plot should inspect both.

Buildings are also recorded in the **cadastre**. Whether and when a new building must be surveyed is governed by the survey and cadastre laws of the federal states; for temporary fixtures it depends on the state rule, which you can ask the competent survey or cadastre office about.

## Worked example: container on leased land

A business leases a plot for eight years and has a fitted-out container installed. The figures are **assumptions** for illustration, not market prices:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase including fit-out | €14,000 |
| Removal and transport at end of contract | €900 |
| Expected resale proceeds | €4,000 |
| **Net capital consumed** (14,000 + 900 − 4,000) | **€10,900** |
| Per year (÷ 8) | approx. €1,363 |
| Per month (÷ 96) | approx. €114 |

If ownership were lost instead – for example because the container counts as a permanent part of the land and the contract says nothing else – the resale proceeds would fall away. Then €14,000 of purchase cost would have no return: approx. €146 per month (14,000 ÷ 96) instead of approx. €114. The difference of roughly €32 per month, or €3,100 over the term, shows what a clean contract clause is worth (removal costs are not included in this case).

## Lease, hire and the landowner

Anyone who places a container as a lessee or tenant should record in the contract:

- The container stays the **lessee's property** and is attached to the ground only for a temporary purpose (section 95 BGB).
- Who bears **removal and restoration**, and by when?
- Is there a **right of removal** or a pre-emption right for the lessor over the container?
- How does a **sale of the plot** affect the lease? For leases, section 581(2) BGB applies the tenant protection rules on sale of the property (section 566 BGB) accordingly; the buyer then steps into the contract. In individual cases this can depend on the form of the contract.
- Who insures the container? See [insuring a container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern).

For hire containers owned by the lessor the same applies in reverse: the container belongs to the lessor and the hirer may not build it in permanently; details in [rental contract and law](/en/ratgeber/container-mietvertrag-recht).

## Checklist before placing a container

- [ ] Ownership of the plot, or written consent of the owner?
- [ ] Purpose and duration of placement recorded in writing?
- [ ] Detachable connection planned (foundation type, services)?
- [ ] Duty to remove, right of removal and costs settled in the contract?
- [ ] Land register extract and, where applicable, building encumbrance register inspected?
- [ ] Planning-law position clarified – permit as a permanent structure or time-limited? See [building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung)
- [ ] Tax classification agreed with a tax adviser (movable asset or building)?
- [ ] Invoice, photos and contract secured as proof of ownership?
- [ ] If financed: retention of title and bank security coordinated?

## Important note

This article gives general guidance on German civil law and is not legal advice. Whether a container is a temporary fixture or an essential component depends on location, duration, attachment and use in each case; planning-law admissibility follows its own rules, see [building permit](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung). For a land purchase, a longer lease or financing, consult a lawyer or notary. The calculated figures are assumptions without guarantee.
