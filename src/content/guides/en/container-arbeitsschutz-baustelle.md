## Who is responsible

On a construction site, several levels of responsibility meet. Who answers for what is often the first point of dispute – and can only be sketched broadly, because it depends on the actual contracts.

- **The employer** is responsible for the occupational safety of their employees, including suitable break, changing and sanitary facilities. The basis lies in the German Occupational Safety Act and the **Workplaces Ordinance (Arbeitsstättenverordnung, ArbStättV)**, made concrete by the **Technical Rules for Workplaces (ASR)**.
- **The client or commissioning party** has separate duties under the **Construction Site Ordinance (Baustellenverordnung, BaustellV)**: advance notification for larger sites, appointing a **coordinator (SiGeKo)** and, depending on the case, a health and safety plan.
- **The coordinator** aligns the measures of several firms, but does not replace the responsibility of each individual employer.
- **The employers' liability insurance association for the construction industry (BG BAU)** is the accident insurer responsible for advice, supervision and the **DGUV rules**. Its inspectors check implementation on site.

> The regulations named here provide orientation. What is actually required on a specific site depends on size, duration, headcount, the risk assessment and the requirements of the competent authorities.

## Construction Site Ordinance: when additional duties apply

The Workplaces Ordinance applies to every site with employees. The German Construction Site Ordinance (BaustellV) also places duties on the client that depend on the size and make-up of the site. This matters for container planning, because the health and safety plan (SiGe-Plan) and the site rules often specify where the welfare containers stand and who provides them.

| Situation | Duty under BaustellV | What it means for the containers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expected to last more than 30 working days **and** more than 20 workers at the same time | Advance notice to the competent authority at least two weeks before the site is set up, notice displayed on site | The site is large enough that the scope and location of welfare facilities should be settled early |
| Expected to exceed 500 person-days | Advance notice as well | As above – even with few workers over a long period |
| Employees of several employers on site | Appointment of one or more coordinators (SiGeKo) | Clarify whether welfare containers are shared or provided by each firm |
| Several employers **and** advance notice required or particularly hazardous work | Health and safety plan in addition | Location, access and use of the container set-up are sensibly recorded in the plan |

A worked example of the person-day threshold: four workers over 130 working days add up to 520 person-days – advance notice is therefore due, even though never more than four people work at the same time. The thresholds are taken from the text of the ordinance; the current version is always what counts.

Where containers are shared, a written arrangement pays off: who rents, who cleans, who heats, who is liable for damage? Without one, each firm may end up setting up its own units – or none at all.

## Which facilities are needed

Construction sites are workplaces. Even if they last only a few weeks, workers need ways to change, wash, take a break and use a toilet. Containers are the usual solution.

| Facility | Purpose | Orientation (guide value) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Break / welfare room** | Recovery away from the work area, shelter from weather, meal breaks | ASR A4.2 deals with break and on-call rooms; the need grows with headcount and duration |
| **Changing room** | Separation of work and private clothing, lockable storage | Separate use for women and men should be provided; drying facilities for wet clothing matter |
| **Washing facilities** | Hand washing, showers for heavily soiling work | ASR A4.1 deals with sanitary rooms; hot water is required for certain activities |
| **Toilets** | Basic need, hygienic and with hand washing | Numbers are based on headcount; short walking distances are the goal |
| **First aid area** | Initial treatment, storage of dressings, chain of rescue | Signage, means of raising the alarm and accessibility are essential |

All entries in this table are **guide values for orientation**. The binding requirements follow from the current version of the ArbStättV and the ASR, and from the employer's risk assessment.

## Numbers and sizes: orders of magnitude

How many units are required cannot be stated flatly. The rules work with reference values such as headcount, simultaneous use and type of activity. The following gives **orders of magnitude for orientation**, not binding requirements.

| Workers (simultaneously) | Order of magnitude, break area | Order of magnitude, sanitary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| up to approx. 5 | one small welfare container may suffice | toilet cubicle with hand washing |
| approx. 6–20 | one to two welfare containers, separate changing room sensible | several toilets, washing area with several taps |
| approx. 20–50 | several units, possibly coupled into larger areas | sanitary container with showers for heavily soiling work |
| more than approx. 50 | container complex over several levels or rows | several sanitary units, toilets and washrooms spread for short walking distances |

On separation by gender: toilets, washrooms and changing rooms must in principle be provided separately for women and men, or separate use must be made possible – for example by staggered use in small teams. ASR A4.1 sets out in detail when which solution is acceptable.

For break rooms, ASR A4.2 gives as orientation a floor area of **at least approx. 1 m² per person using the room at the same time**, including seat and table, and a **minimum room size of approx. 6 m²**. There are further requirements on room height, daylight and a view outside. What counts is the current text of the ASR and the assessment of the individual case.

## Worked example: welfare containers for 14 workers

Assume a site with **14 workers taking their break at the same time** and a construction period of **eight months**. The areas are taken from [Container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse) (insulated portable cabins, approximate values, manufacturer-dependent).

**Step 1 – break room area.** 14 persons × 1 m² = 14 m². A single 20 ft portable cabin offers approx. 5.85 × 2.23 m inside, i.e. approx. 13.0 m² – and the area behind the door, at the heater and by the window still has to come off that. So it is not enough here.

| Option | Usable area approx. | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 × 20 ft standard width | 13.0 m² | too small for 14 people |
| 1 × 20 ft XL (3.00 m wide) | 16.3 m² | just sufficient, but an oversize transport requiring a permit |
| 2 × 20 ft, coupled along the long side | approx. 26 m² | sufficient, room for a drying corner and coat rack, easy to transport |

**Step 2 – complete set-up.** Chosen: two coupled 20 ft office containers as the break room, a third 20 ft office container as the changing room and one 20 ft sanitary container. Monthly rents are taken from [Container rental prices](/en/ratgeber/container-mietpreise-kalkulation), transport prices from [Transport and delivery costs](/en/ratgeber/container-transport-lieferkosten) (lorry with loading crane, local area, per trip).

| Item | Assumption | Cost approx. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 × office container 20 ft, insulated | approx. €150–350 / month each | €450–1,050 / month |
| 1 × sanitary container 20 ft | approx. €250–700 / month | €250–700 / month |
| **Rent per month** | | **€700–1,750** |
| Rent over 8 months | 8 × €700 to 8 × €1,750 | €5,600–14,000 |
| Delivery and collection | 8 trips by lorry with loading crane at approx. €350–800 (one trip each way per container) | €2,800–6,400 |
| **Total rent and transport** | | **approx. €8,400–20,400** |

Not included are site power, water and waste water connections, base, coupling material and assembly, furniture, cleaning, heating electricity and the deposit. Conversely, the usual long-term discount has not been deducted: for six to twelve months, the rental price guide puts the negotiable discount at approx. 10–25 % of the monthly rent, in this example roughly €560 (10 % of the lower figure) to €3,500 (25 % of the upper figure). If one lorry can bring two containers in a single trip, transport costs fall accordingly.

The range is wide because equipment, region and supplier vary a great deal. A reliable budget requires itemised quotes; how to compare them is explained in [Comparing quotes](/en/ratgeber/container-angebote-vergleichen). The transport side can be estimated for your own distance with the [transport cost calculator](/en/rechner/transportkosten).

Further guidance on selecting and equipping sanitary units is in [Sanitary containers](/en/ratgeber/sanitaercontainer-ratgeber).

## Equipment in practice

A container only serves its purpose with suitable equipment. The following basics have proven themselves:

- **Heating:** Sufficient capacity for the cold season; break rooms should genuinely allow workers to warm up.
- **Lighting:** Adequate artificial lighting, ideally supplemented by daylight through windows.
- **Ventilation:** Openable windows or mechanical ventilation – important against moisture, odours and condensation.
- **Seating and tables:** Enough seats with backrests and table surfaces for breaks.
- **Drying facilities for clothing:** A key point for outdoor work; wet clothing must dry before the next shift.
- **Drinking water:** Ensure access to drinking water or beverages, especially in hot weather.
- **Lockers:** For personal belongings and separate storage of work and street clothing.
- **Hygiene equipment:** Soap, towels or dryers, waste bins, regular cleaning.

Practical notes on procurement – rental period, delivery, handover protocol – are in [Renting a container: the process](/en/ratgeber/container-mieten-ablauf).

## Electrical safety and inspections

Site power is a classic inspection topic. Containers are almost always supplied electrically, often via temporary cabling.

- **Site distribution boards:** Supply through suitable boards approved for site use is standard. They must be positioned protected from the weather and secured against mechanical damage.
- **Residual current devices (RCD):** Residual current protection is part of the standard protective measures on construction sites.
- **Cables:** Use only suitable, undamaged cables; protect them against being driven over and against trip hazards.
- **Recurring inspections:** Portable and fixed electrical equipment is subject to recurring inspection by competent persons. Intervals depend on conditions of use and the risk assessment.
- **Inspection records:** Document results and keep them available on site – during a check, that is the first evidence.

The specific requirements and intervals follow from the Industrial Safety Ordinance, the DGUV rules and recognised engineering practice. Advice from the BG BAU is the most practical starting point. How connecting a container to the grid generally works and who may do it is explained in [Power connection for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).

## Siting and structural stability

The way units are sited decides safety and usability – and is frequently underestimated.

- **Load-bearing ground:** The ground must take the loads without the container settling or twisting. Point loads at the corners matter; a twisted container has jamming doors.
- **Levelling:** Level siting is a precondition for functioning doors, windows and drains.
- **Anchoring against wind:** Empty and elevated units are vulnerable to wind. Anchoring, ballasting or coupling may be required.
- **Stacking:** Only in line with the manufacturer's specifications, with suitable connectors, stairs and railings. The basics are in [Stacking and coupling containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln).
- **Escape and rescue routes:** Doors must remain freely accessible, routes marked and lit; no storage in front of exits.
- **Distances and fire protection:** Keep distance from neighbouring buildings, storage areas and hazardous substances; provide and mark fire extinguishers.
- **Accessibility:** Short, safe routes from the work area, separation from vehicle traffic, proper stairs instead of improvised steps.
- **Traffic safety:** Lit walkways, slip-resistant surfaces, fall protection where units are elevated.

For choosing the base – timber sleepers, concrete slabs, strip or pad foundations – see [Container foundations](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament). For protection against burglary and vandalism outside working hours, see [Securing a container](/en/ratgeber/container-sichern-diebstahlschutz).

## Permits and location of the site set-up

Containers set up as site facilities only for the duration of construction work are exempt from the permit procedure under many German state building codes. That is not a blanket exemption, however: it depends on the state building code concerned, the duration and the use. Distances, fire protection and escape routes must be observed even for exempt units, and anyone who leaves containers in place beyond the construction period or uses them differently leaves the scope of a site facility. An overview is given in [Building permits for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

If the set-up stands wholly or partly on a pavement or road, a special-use permit or traffic order is required regardless of building law – details in [Containers on public land](/en/ratgeber/container-oeffentlicher-grund-sondernutzung). If workers stay overnight on or next to the site, separate requirements apply to the accommodation (ASR A4.4) that go beyond those for a break room.

## Winter operation

The cold season adds points that should be planned ahead:

- **Frost protection for sanitary units:** Drain pipes and tanks or operate them frost-safe, using trace heating or frost monitors.
- **Heating:** Sufficient output, safe positioning of heaters, adequate ventilation – improvised heating is a frequent cause of fires.
- **Slip hazards:** Clear and grit accesses, stairs and platforms; a gritting plan prevents disputes after an incident.
- **Drying options:** Particularly important in winter so that work clothing dries before the next shift.
- **Condensation:** Regular ventilation prevents moisture damage and mould in insulated units.

More on frost protection, heating and moisture in [Containers in winter](/en/ratgeber/container-im-winter); on extinguishers, heaters and distances in [Fire safety for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-brandschutz-vorschriften).

## Checklist before setting up

These questions should be answered before ordering – they determine the number, type and location of the containers:

- [ ] How many workers are on site **at the same time**, and how many take their break together?
- [ ] How long will the site run – and does the BaustellV advance notice apply (more than 30 working days and more than 20 workers at the same time, or more than 500 person-days)?
- [ ] Are several firms working on site? Who provides, operates and cleans the welfare containers?
- [ ] Is there heavily soiling work that requires showers and hot water?
- [ ] Are separate toilets, washing and changing facilities – or their separate use – organised?
- [ ] Is there a location with load-bearing, level ground and access for a lorry with loading crane?
- [ ] Is site power with a suitable site distribution board, water and waste water within reach?
- [ ] Is the location on private land, or is a special-use permit required?
- [ ] Are escape and rescue routes, fire extinguishers and first aid materials planned?
- [ ] Is winter operation (frost protection, heating, gritting plan) arranged if the works run into the cold season?
- [ ] Are itemised quotes available for rent, transport, coupling and deposit?

## Documentation and monitoring

In occupational safety, much depends on being able to prove it:

1. **Prepare and maintain the risk assessment** – the starting point for almost all measures.
2. **Document instruction sessions** for employees, including emergency behaviour.
3. **Collect inspection records** for electrical equipment, ladders, scaffolding and heaters.
4. **Define cleaning and maintenance intervals** for sanitary and welfare units, and document them.
5. **Carry out regular site walks**, recording defects with a deadline and a responsible person.
6. **Display contacts** for emergencies, first aiders and the coordinator visibly.

## Important note

> This article contains **general information** and does **not constitute legal advice**. Only the **applicable regulations** are binding – in particular the Occupational Safety Act, the Workplaces Ordinance (ArbStättV) with the Technical Rules for Workplaces (ASR), the Construction Site Ordinance (BaustellV), the Industrial Safety Ordinance and the relevant **DGUV rules** – each in its current version. The areas, quantities and equipment features named here are deliberately marked as **guide values and orders of magnitude** and do not replace an assessment of the individual case. Binding information is given by the competent **occupational safety authority** and the **employers' liability insurance association for the construction industry (BG BAU)**; when in doubt, consult an occupational safety specialist or a lawyer.

## Conclusion

Break, changing and sanitary facilities on construction sites are not a comfort feature but an occupational safety requirement. Containers are the practical answer – provided number, size and equipment match the headcount and the type of work. The worked example shows how quickly a single container becomes too small: for 14 people, one 20 ft portable cabin is not enough as a break room, and a small set-up of four units costs approx. €8,400–20,400 over eight months including transport – before connections and incidental costs. Larger sites and those with several firms also trigger duties under the Construction Site Ordinance. The ArbStättV and the ASR provide orientation, for example ASR A4.1 for sanitary rooms and ASR A4.2 for break and on-call rooms; binding are the current version of those rules and the assessment of the individual case. Planning early saves rework: load-bearing ground, a safe electrical supply with documented inspections, clear escape routes and a well thought-out winter setup are far easier to implement before work starts. When in doubt, contacting the BG BAU or the occupational safety authority is the fastest route – and the only one that gives binding information.
