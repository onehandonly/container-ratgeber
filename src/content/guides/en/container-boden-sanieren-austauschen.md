Anyone buying or converting a used container usually looks at the walls and roof first. The floor gets less attention, yet it takes the heaviest mechanical load (forklifts, pallets, machinery), absorbs moisture from above and is exposed to splash water and standing damp from below. This guide helps you assess its condition, choose the right measure and estimate the cost. Building and workplace rules referred to are German; costs are in euros.

## How a container floor is built

A typical shipping container has a wooden-board floor, usually around 28 mm thick (plywood, sometimes hardwood or bamboo), resting on steel floor cross members. The cross members run across the container and connect to the two bottom side rails, which together with the corner posts form the load-bearing structure. The boards are laid on top and fixed with screws; they carry the cargo and pass it into the cross members.

This gives an important distinction:

- **The board floor** is a wearing part. It can be replaced without touching the container's structure.
- **Cross members and side rails** are part of the structure. Rust-through or deformation there is not cosmetic but a job for a proper welding repair (see [rust protection and care](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz)).

Exact board thickness and material differ by manufacturer and year of build; what counts is what you find on your container.

## Spotting damage: an inspection routine

Walk the floor twice – from above and from below.

**From above (inside):**

1. Probe joints and edges with a screwdriver or knife: soft, fibrous wood indicates rot.
2. Walk across the floor and listen for springy or creaking spots – boards may have sagged or fixings may have come loose.
3. Swollen board edges, dark water marks and a musty smell point to repeated wetting.
4. Note oil, grease and chemical stains; they may be cargo residue and affect later use.
5. Check for protruding screw heads and torn-out fixings, especially near the doors where forklifts load hardest.

**From below (underside):**

1. Inspect cross members for blistering rust, pitting and rust-through, especially at the supports and near the doors.
2. Look for visible deformation (sagging).
3. Look for moisture traces on the underside of the boards.
4. Assess previous repairs and welded-on plates: are they cleanly made and painted?

If you are inspecting before a purchase, further checkpoints are in [buying a used container](/en/ratgeber/gebrauchten-container-kaufen). Photograph defects – they support price negotiation and document the condition at handover.

## Health: what to clarify about wood treatment

Floor boards are sometimes treated during manufacture against moisture and pests. Which agents were used depends on manufacturer and year. For pure storage this rarely matters; if you want to live, work or store food in the container, do not leave it to chance:

- Ask the dealer in writing about the origin and treatment of the floor.
- If unclear, assume the residues are unknown and, for living or office use, plan either a replacement or a sealed overlay with a vapour barrier.
- Ventilate a used container thoroughly before entering if there is a noticeable smell; containers may previously have carried unusual cargo.

A guide cannot give a legally robust assessment here; if in doubt, consult a specialist in indoor pollutants.

## The options at a glance

| Measure | Suitable for | Effort | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Replace individual boards or areas | Local damage, cross members intact | low to medium | Mind joints and height offsets |
| Seal and coat the floor | Intact boards, storage use, binding oil/dust | low | Does not fix rot or cross-member damage |
| Lay a new build-up over the old floor | Living use, sealed floor build-up wanted | medium | Costs interior height; moisture below must be ruled out |
| Replace the board floor completely | Rot, unknown treatment, residential/office conversion | medium to high | Time; cross members must be repaired first |
| Steel floor (e.g. checker plate) | Heavy loads, workshops | high | Heavy, cold, needs insulation |

The key point: **fix the cause first, then renew the floor.** Laying new boards on cross members that keep rusting, or in a container that collects condensation, only postpones the damage.

## Replacement step by step

A full replacement is feasible for practically skilled people; for welding and structural parts, involve a specialist firm.

1. **Remove the old floor:** release or drill out fixings, lift the boards. Mind disposal: depending on treatment, the wood may be classified differently as waste; ask the disposal company.
2. **De-rust the cross members:** mechanically down to sound steel, then rust-protection primer and paint. Have rusted-through members welded before relaying.
3. **Clarify drainage and condensation:** clear the drain holes, check the seals, plan ventilation ([ventilation in containers](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung)).
4. **Cut the new boards:** the interior of a 20-foot container is approx. 5.90 × 2.35 m, i.e. about 14 m². Standard sheets (e.g. 2.44 × 1.22 m) must be cut; allow for waste and place the joints over cross members.
5. **Fix:** rust-protected, countersunk screws; seal joints and board edges against moisture.
6. **Insulation and floor build-up** only afterwards, if residential or office use is planned ([insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen)).

The example shows that a used container with a damaged floor quickly costs several hundred to over a thousand euros extra. Factor this in when comparing with a near-new container – a container with a repaired or new floor can win on total price, whereas a bargain with a rotten floor can become expensive. ## Which floor for which use?

| Use | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Pure storage (garden tools, boxes) | An intact standard floor is enough; keep moisture away |
| Workshop with machinery | Inspect the floor, use load-spreading supports, see [workshop & garage](/en/ratgeber/container-werkstatt-garage) |
| Storage with forklift | Check board condition and door area closely, ask the supplier for load figures |
| Office, living | Replace the floor if treatment is unknown; sealed, insulated build-up |
| Food, catering | Clarify treatment; cleanable, suitable covering |

Floor coverings such as vinyl or click laminate belong on a dry, level substrate, not directly on unassessed old boards.

## Typical mistakes

- **Inspecting only the top:** the underside reveals whether the cross members are sound.
- **Laying new boards on a damp base:** this causes new damage within a short time.
- **Insulating the floor without a vapour barrier and ventilation concept:** condensation forms on the underside, see [thermal bridges](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container).
- **Forgetting interior height:** a thick floor build-up (approx. 60–100 mm) costs headroom, noticeably more in a standard container than in a high cube.
- **Poor ground:** an uneven container twists; provide a proper [foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).

## Important note

This article is general orientation, not professional design. For load-bearing steel parts, welding and suspected contaminants, engage specialist firms. Residential and workplace use are subject to German building and workplace regulations; whether and what you need approved depends on location, duration and use – see [containers and building permits](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

## Conclusion

The floor is a replaceable wearing part – as long as the structure beneath it is intact. Inspect from above and below, clarify the wood treatment for living or office use, and remove moisture causes before relaying. For storage, a partial repair is often enough; if you plan a conversion, budget the floor as a separate cost item from the start.
