When space runs out, a storage container is often the most pragmatic answer. It needs no construction time, no conventional foundation and no utility connections. A hook-lift (roll-off) truck or a lorry with a loader crane delivers and sets it down, and shortly afterwards you have lockable, weatherproof space. The real work happens beforehand: deciding which size, design and condition suit the purpose. A badly chosen container costs more than money. It causes follow-on problems, from too little space and damp goods to doors that no longer lock properly on uneven ground.

## What storage containers deliver

A storage container based on a shipping container is a steel box with its own structural frame. The load is carried by a steel frame of four corner posts plus floor and roof side rails; the eight corner castings, four at the top and four at the bottom, are its connection points. The profiled wall and roof panels brace this frame and seal it. That is what sets it apart from metal garages or garden sheds, whose shells are built far more lightly.

Three properties make it attractive for storage:

- **Material.** The shell is usually weathering steel (Corten), which is additionally painted. Corten is not stainless: where the paint is damaged and water stands, it rusts. With a well-maintained coating, however, the shell withstands knocks and weather for many years. Maintenance tips are in [Care and rust protection](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz).
- **Locking.** The door leaves are held by locking bars that engage the frame at top and bottom. A leaf cannot easily be levered open; the weak point is almost always the lock, not the structure.
- **Weather resistance.** Rubber seals around the doors and a welded shell make an intact container rainproof. There are no drainage holes in the floor. That also means moisture that gets in stays in.

> Rule of thumb: a sound storage container protects against water from outside. Only proper ventilation protects against water from inside, in other words condensation.

The floor of a shipping container is usually plywood about 28 mm thick (hardwood, sometimes bamboo) on steel cross members. It is designed for heavy loads but is sensitive to standing water. How to assess and replace a damaged floor is covered in [Repairing a container floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen).

## Sizes at a glance

The 10, 20 and 40 ft lengths are ISO-standardised; 8 ft and 15 ft storage containers are special formats whose internal dimensions depend on the manufacturer. Figures are rounded approximations for standard height (approx. 2.39 m inside).

| Size | External approx. (L × W) | Internal approx. (L × W) | Floor area approx. | Volume approx. | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **8 ft** | 2.44 × 2.20 m | manufacturer-specific | 4–5 m² | approx. 10 m³ | Garden tools, equipment, club supplies |
| **10 ft** | 2.99 × 2.44 m | 2.83 × 2.35 m | 6.7 m² | 16 m³ | Site materials, tradesperson's store |
| **15 ft** | 4.55 × 2.44 m | manufacturer-specific | 9–10 m² | approx. 20–24 m³ | In-between size where space is tight |
| **20 ft** | 6.06 × 2.44 m | 5.90 × 2.35 m | 13.9 m² | 33 m³ | Standard size for household goods, materials, machinery |
| **40 ft** | 12.19 × 2.44 m | 12.03 × 2.35 m | 28.3 m² | 67 m³ | Large stores, furniture, exhibition kit, production buffer |

The 20 ft container is the standard size for good reason: common vehicles can deliver it, it fits on many plots and it covers much of what private and business users need. A 40 ft container, by contrast, needs an articulated lorry or low-loader, a firm, wide access route and plenty of manoeuvring space.

Besides standard height (approx. 2.59 m outside, 2.39 m inside) there is the high cube version at approx. 2.90 m outside and 2.70 m inside. That raises the volume to approx. 37 m³ for a 20 ft and approx. 76 m³ for a 40 ft unit. The extra 30-odd centimetres pay off if you stack high or might later fit insulation. An often overlooked bottleneck is the door opening: approx. 2.34 × 2.28 m (high cube approx. 2.58 m high), slightly smaller than the interior. All dimensions in detail: [Container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse).

## Calculate your needs rather than guess

The most common mistake is a container that looks big enough on paper but proves too small in daily use. The reason: a store that stays usable can never be filled to 100 per cent. Aisle, door area and clearance below the roof all take space.

**Volume example.** Suppose you want to store approx. 18 m³ of goods and be able to reach all of it. With a central aisle, a fill rate of around 60–70 per cent is realistic. You then need an internal volume of 18 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 26 m³ to 18 ÷ 0.6 = 30 m³. A 15 ft container would be too tight; a 20 ft standard container with approx. 33 m³ fits, with a little reserve. If you only ever load and rarely retrieve, you can pack more densely; if you rearrange often, work with 60 per cent.

**Pallet example.** Euro pallets measure 1.20 × 0.80 m. With an internal width of approx. 2.35 m, two pallets will not fit side by side crosswise (2 × 1.20 = 2.40 m), but one lengthwise and one crosswise will (0.80 + 1.20 = 2.00 m). In a 20 ft container that gives 4 pallets in one row (4 × 1.20 = 4.80 m) and 7 in the other (7 × 0.80 = 5.60 m), 11 floor positions in total. A 40 ft container allows up to 25 in theory (10 + 15), but with almost no tolerance, so fewer are realistic in practice depending on how you load. Pallet-wide containers with approx. 2.44 m internal width take 15 pallets in 20 ft format but are less common on the used market. Note: with pallets and no aisle, you can only reach the front row from the door.

How much extra shelf area you can gain, and how to plan aisle and shelf levels, is worked through in [Using a storage container properly](/en/ratgeber/lagercontainer-richtig-nutzen). For your own dimensions, use the [volume calculator](/en/rechner/container-volumen).

## One 40 ft container or two 20 ft containers?

If you need around 28 m², you face a real choice. Floor area is almost the same (approx. 28.3 m² versus 2 × 13.9 = 27.8 m²), the consequences are not. Prices follow the ranges further down, used units:

| Criterion | 1 × 40 ft | 2 × 20 ft |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchase price used approx. | €2,500 – 4,500 | €3,000 – 6,000 |
| Purchase price per m² of floor approx. | €88 – 159 | €108 – 216 |
| Delivery (local area) | one articulated lorry trip approx. €400–900 plus crane | two trips, e.g. hook-lift truck approx. €180–350 each |
| Access and site | long, firm access, large turning circle | more flexible, can be placed separately |
| Access to goods | one end door, back hard to reach | two doors, shorter distances |
| Organisation | one space, one lock | two separate areas, e.g. per user or product group |
| Resale | smaller market | 20 ft is the most common format |

Per square metre the 40 ft container is usually cheaper. Whether that advantage survives often depends on the site: if the lorry cannot unload directly, a separately booked mobile crane is added, costing approx. €600–1,800 per day according to [Container transport and delivery costs](/en/ratgeber/container-transport-lieferkosten). In that case two 20 ft containers are often the simpler and no more expensive solution.

## Designs compared

Not every storage container is a converted shipping container. Several construction principles compete on the market.

| Design | Construction | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shipping container as store** | ISO steel container, steel frame with profiled walls, double doors at one end | Robust, stackable, widely available, inexpensive | Heavy, uninsulated, prone to condensation, only one door |
| **Modular material container** | Frame with inserted wall elements, sometimes sandwich panels | Lighter, often better door options, special lengths | Usually less stackable, more sensitive to impact |
| **Tunnel container** | Doors at both ends | Load-through possible, good cross-ventilation | Smaller supply, higher price |
| **Container with side door** | Additional door in the long wall | Short distances, good for shelving | Higher price |
| **Open side** | Entire long side opens | Bulky goods, exhibitions | Considerably more expensive, rarely used |

For pure storage without later conversion, the classic shipping container is usually the most economical choice. If goods go in and out frequently, a side door or second end door can be worthwhile because it saves rearranging to reach items at the back. Doors can be added later, but this alters the shell; see [Retrofitting windows and doors](/en/ratgeber/container-fenster-tueren-nachruesten).

## Condition grades and quality

There is no official standard on the used market, but there are established terms. Understanding them lets you compare offers more realistically.

- **As new / one-trip.** The container has been shipped once, loaded, and then counts as used. It looks almost new; seals and paint are intact.
- **WWT (wind and watertight).** The usual standard for storage: signs of use, dents and surface rust, but tight.
- **Cargo worthy (CW).** Also suitable for sea transport. Usually unnecessary for storage on land; only worthwhile if the container is to be shipped again. What the CSC plate tells you is explained in [Container number and CSC plate](/en/ratgeber/container-nummer-csc-plakette).
- **As is / in need of repair.** Cheap, but with rust holes, faulty doors or damaged floors. Only sensible if you can realistically estimate repair costs.

These terms are seller descriptions, not guarantees. On inspection, what counts is the floor (soft spots, stains), door seals (cracks, brittleness), corner posts and floor rails (rust holes) and how smoothly the locking bars move. A look from inside with the doors closed reveals holes immediately; there should be no points of light. To tick off on site there is the [inspection checklist](/en/rechner/besichtigung-checkliste); in detail: [Inspection and test report](/en/ratgeber/container-besichtigung-pruefprotokoll). At delivery itself, the [acceptance report](/en/ratgeber/container-anlieferung-abnahmeprotokoll) helps.

## Ventilation and condensation

Warm, humid air cools on the steel roof at night, drops below the dew point and condenses as water. This condensation is a frequent cause of damage to stored goods and has nothing to do with leaks.

The most effective measures are: store goods dry, fit vents diagonally offset where possible (low on one side, high on the opposite side), keep goods away from walls and floor, and insulate the roof for sensitive goods. The background and the suitability of different goods are covered in [Using a storage container properly](/en/ratgeber/lagercontainer-richtig-nutzen). Batteries, fuels and chemicals are subject to their own rules; see [Storing batteries and hazardous substances in a container](/en/ratgeber/container-akkus-gefahrstoffe-lagern).

## Accessories and fit-out

A storage container straight from the depot is an empty box. Accessories turn it into a working store.

- **Shelving.** Heavy-duty shelving along the long walls multiplies the storage surface. If the container is to be moved later, free-standing or removably fixed shelving is more practical than shelving bolted in permanently.
- **Vents.** Retrofit vents with insect mesh are cheap and work immediately.
- **Lighting.** Without mains power, battery LED lights with motion sensors help. A fixed connection belongs in the hands of a qualified electrician; see [Power connection](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).
- **Floor protection.** An extra layer or covering protects the original floor from wear and spilled liquids.
- **Ramp.** A loading ramp makes it much easier to use a pallet truck or sack truck, because the floor sits higher than the surrounding ground on its rails and supports.

## Security

The weak point is the lock. An exposed padlock shackle gives tools something to grip. A lock box that encloses the shackle makes this much harder. Further measures include bar locks, positioning the doors facing a wall or a neighbouring container, and lighting and visibility. Details in [Securing a container](/en/ratgeber/container-sichern-diebstahlschutz); whether and how the container and contents are insured is covered in [Insuring a container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern).

## Buying or renting: price overview

All figures are rough approximate ranges (as of 2026, German market) without guarantee. They depend on condition, region and market conditions and are not an offer. For 20 and 40 ft they match the figures in [What does a container cost?](/en/ratgeber/was-kostet-ein-container) and [Rental prices and calculation](/en/ratgeber/container-mietpreise-kalkulation).

| Size | Purchase used (WWT) approx. | Purchase as new / one-trip approx. | Rent per month approx. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **8 ft** | rarely available | new approx. €2,500 – 3,800 | €50 – 90 |
| **10 ft** | €1,500 – 2,600 | €2,800 – 4,200 | €50 – 90 |
| **15 ft** | rarely available | new approx. €3,200 – 4,800 | €75 – 130 |
| **20 ft** | €1,500 – 3,000 | €3,000 – 5,000 | €70 – 130 |
| **40 ft** | €2,500 – 4,500 | €4,500 – 7,000 | €110 – 200 |

Small formats cost noticeably more relative to their floor area, because manufacturing, transport and administration are hardly cheaper than for a 20 ft container.

**Additional costs.** In the local area a single trip costs, depending on the vehicle, approx. €120–250 with a skip loader (only up to about 10 ft), approx. €180–350 with a hook-lift truck, and approx. €350–800 with a lorry with loader crane; 40 ft containers usually come by articulated lorry for approx. €400–900 plus crane. With rental, delivery and collection are each paid once, often plus a deposit and cleaning fee. The [transport cost estimator](/en/rechner/transportkosten) gives an estimate for your case.

**Worked example.** Used 20 ft storage container, purchase approx. €2,400, monthly rent approx. €95, delivery and collection by hook-lift truck approx. €250 each:

| Point in time | Purchase (€2,400 + €250 delivery) | Rental (€500 transport + €95 per month) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| after 12 months | approx. €2,650 | approx. €1,640 |
| after 24 months | approx. €2,650 | approx. €2,780 |

Without resale, buying pays off after around 23 months: (2,650 − 500) ÷ 95 ≈ 22.6. If the container is later sold for an assumed €1,700 and another approx. €250 is spent on collection, the break-even falls to around 8 months: (2,650 + 250 − 1,700 − 500) ÷ 95 ≈ 7.4. The sale price is uncertain, though. Realistically, the threshold for simple storage containers therefore lies somewhere between about six months and two years. The full comparison is in [Buy or rent a container?](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufen-oder-mieten), and the [Buy or rent? calculator](/en/rechner/kaufen-oder-mieten) works with your own figures. If you only need a few cubic metres for a few months, also compare [Self-storage or storage container](/en/ratgeber/self-storage-oder-lagercontainer).

## Site and installation

The ground determines service life. A container should not sit flat on soil: moisture under the floor rails encourages rust. Common solutions are point supports made of concrete slabs or strip foundations under the corner castings, with additional intermediate supports for 40 ft units. What the ground has to carry and which bases are suitable is covered in [Container foundations](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).

A level base matters. If the container is twisted, the doors often no longer lock properly. A very slight, deliberate fall can help rainwater run off the roof instead of standing, but it should be only a few millimetres and must not twist the frame.

Whether a permit is required in Germany depends on the federal state, location, how long the container stays and what it is used for; there is no blanket answer. An overview is in [Container planning permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung), and boundary distances are covered in [Neighbour law and boundary distances](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand).

## Selection checklist

- What volume do you need, calculated with a fill rate of around 60–70 % rather than 100 %?
- Will your needs grow, so that 20 ft soon becomes too small?
- Will goods go in and out frequently? Then consider an aisle, a side door or two smaller containers.
- Does the largest single item fit through the door opening (approx. 2.34 × 2.28 m)?
- Are the goods sensitive to moisture or temperature? Then plan ventilation and insulation from the outset.
- How long will you need the container, and is resale realistic?
- Which vehicle can reach the site, and will a crane be needed?
- Will the ground bear the load, and can a level base be created?
- Has the condition been checked on site: floor, seals, frame, locking?
- Is a lock box fitted or can one be added?

## Conclusion

Storage containers are among the cheapest forms of extra, quickly available space. Choosing one is less about the supplier than about an honest calculation of your needs: if you estimate volume with a realistic fill rate, how often you need access and how sensitive the goods are, and check the site access, you will usually arrive at the right size. In many cases that is a used, wind and watertight 20 ft container on level point supports with vents and a lock box. If you need around 28 m², compare one 40 ft unit with two 20 ft units directly, including transport. Whether buying or renting is cheaper is, in this price range, usually decided within the first two years.
