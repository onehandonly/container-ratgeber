Hardly anyone thinks about the roof of a container until it drips inside. Then the questions come at once: is it a leak or just condensation, where is the water getting in, and is a coating enough? This guide works through the roof step by step: first construction and load capacity, then drainage, then waterproofing, and finally what you may add on top. General rust care is covered in [Container maintenance and rust protection](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz); here we deal with what is specific to the roof. Legal remarks refer to German law.

## How the roof is built

The roof of a standard shipping container is corrugated steel sheet a few millimetres thick, welded to the surrounding frame. The container's load-bearing structure is the steel frame made of corner posts plus bottom and top side rails; the eight corner fittings are its nodes. The roof sheet itself is mainly a **skin**, not a load-bearing slab.

One detail matters for drainage: the corrugations of the roof run across the container, at right angles to its length. Rainwater therefore runs along the troughs to the long sides. There is no real fall; the roof is practically flat. Even small dents or a slightly sagging position let water stand.

The roof area follows from the external dimensions: for a 20 ft container that is about 6.06 × 2.44 m, roughly 14.8 m². A 40 ft container (about 12.19 m long) has roughly 29.7 m². The weight examples below refer to these areas.

## What the roof is allowed to carry

It pays to distinguish three quantities that are often confused:

1. **Walking on it for maintenance.** The container standard (ISO 1496-1) tests the roof with a point load of 300 kg on a small area of about 600 × 300 mm. That is a test value for a person standing on it, not an area load. Do not scale it up per square metre.
2. **Area loads from snow and add-ons.** For standard containers there is usually no manufacturer figure. In practice roofs often cope with snow, but that is not a calculated proof.
3. **Stacked loads.** When stacking, the load travels through the corner fittings into the corner posts, not through the roof sheet (see [Stacking and coupling containers](/en/ratgeber/container-stapeln-und-koppeln)).

A rough worked example for snow: in Germany the characteristic snow load values start, depending on zone and altitude, at about 0.65 kN/m² (around 65 kg per m²) and rise well above that. On 14.8 m² the lowest value corresponds to roughly one tonne of snow. If you install in snowy locations or add loads to the roof, do not estimate – have a structural engineer or the manufacturer confirm it.

## Where the water gets in

When a container gets wet inside, the cause is rarely the large surfaces; it is the weak points. The most common causes:

- **Rust-through at ponding spots.** Standing water ages the paint and attacks the steel. Corten steel is not stainless, and shipping containers are painted in addition; once the paint has failed at a ponding spot, the sheet keeps rusting from there.
- **Seams and roof corners.** Dirt collects around welds and corners, and the coating is under heavy mechanical stress there.
- **Later penetrations.** Roof vents, cable entries, flue connections and fixing holes each create a new potential leak.
- **Damage from transport and handling.** Dents and cracks after loading, see [Lifting containers correctly](/en/ratgeber/container-richtig-anheben).

### Leak or condensation?

Before you waterproof the roof, establish whether water is actually coming in from outside. Condensation is the more frequent cause of "rain" inside a container (see [Ventilating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung)). Some simple checks help:

| Observation | points more to | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drips or damp patches in dry weather too, especially after cold nights | Condensation | Measure humidity, improve ventilation |
| Wet patch only appears after rain, repeatedly in the same place | Leak | Search the roof above that spot |
| Bright point of light with doors closed in a darkened interior | Hole or open seam | Mark the spot from outside |
| Rust streak or drip trace below a roof seam | Leak at the seam | Expose, clean and inspect the seam |

The light-point test is simple and reliable: stand inside with the doors closed in bright daylight and wait until your eyes adjust. Every point of light in the ceiling marks an opening.

## Which waterproofing suits which case

Waterproofing only lasts on a clean, sound substrate. In general that means removing loose paint and rust mechanically, treating rust spots with a suitable primer, removing dust and grease and observing drying times. If you skimp here, even the best coating will flake off later.

| Method | Suitable for | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rust-protection primer plus roof paint | Storage and site containers without an active leak, prevention | Does not bridge cracks or holes |
| Liquid waterproofing (brush-on, usually plastic-based) | Small holes, seams and edges, entire roof surfaces | Substrate needs thorough preparation; follow the manufacturer's processing and temperature limits |
| Membrane (bitumen or plastic), bonded | Permanently used, insulated containers, conversions | More involved, needs expertise; check compatibility with the substrate with the manufacturer |
| Sheet repair by welding | Rusted-through areas | Specialist firm only; recoat afterwards |
| Roof build-up with fall | Permanent use, living and office conversions | Structure and roof build-up have to match |

### Worked example: roof coating on a 20 ft container

With about 14.8 m² of roof (6.06 × 2.44 m) you get the following rough orientation, without guarantee:

- Material for primer and coating: approx. €15–40 per m², i.e. about €220–590.
- Work by a specialist firm including preparation and material: approx. €40–90 per m², i.e. about €590–1,330.

Prices vary widely with system, region and the condition of the roof. Always ask quotes to describe the substrate preparation; it is the real cost driver and the point where quotes diverge (more in [Comparing container quotes](/en/ratgeber/container-angebote-vergleichen)).

## Draining water deliberately

The less water stands on the roof, the longer any coating lasts. These measures work:

- **Level and stable positioning.** A container standing slightly askew drains water to one side; ponding then forms at the low points. See [Foundations for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).
- **Gutter on the long side.** Since the water runs off to the long sides, a gutter there is the obvious choice. Fix it to frame members where possible and without new holes in the roof sheet, and agree the method of fixing with the manufacturer.
- **Keep the roof clear.** Leaves and dirt hold water and encourage rust. Clean the roof at every inspection.
- **Watch dents.** A dent that keeps water after rain needs filler, coating or repair.

## Roof build-ups: what makes sense?

Gravel, green roof, decking or photovoltaics: every build-up adds weight and stress. The overview below helps with a first assessment.

| Build-up | Approx. weight | On 14.8 m² | Remark |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coating only | negligible | – | Standard solution for storage and site use |
| 5 cm gravel layer | approx. 90 kg/m² (at approx. 1,800 kg/m³) | about 1.3 t | Common on flat roofs; on a container roof only after agreement |
| Extensive green roof | approx. 60–150 kg/m², water-saturated | about 0.9–2.2 t | See also the energy-law note in [Containers and energy law](/en/ratgeber/container-geg-energierecht) |
| Photovoltaics with mounting frame | approx. 15–25 kg/m² incl. frame, without ballast | about 220–370 kg | See [Photovoltaics on a container](/en/ratgeber/photovoltaik-auf-dem-container) |
| Raised roof terrace | high, depending on construction | – | Only with structural design |

The figures are orders of magnitude for comparison, not a basis for design. An empty 20 ft container weighs about 2.2 t; an extra 1 to 2 t on the roof is therefore a significant change to the load distribution, especially during transport and craning. The weight must be taken by the frame, not by the middle of the roof sheet. If you plan a green roof, ask the container's manufacturer and a structural engineer first.

If the roof of a living or office conversion is to be insulated, a lot depends on whether you insulate inside or outside. The basic principle is in [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen) – with external insulation on the roof, the roof sheet becomes the vapour-barrier layer, which changes the build-up planning decisively.

## Getting onto the roof

A container roof is smooth, slippery when wet, frosty or dewy, and more uneven than it looks because of the corrugations. Add the fall height of about 2.6 m (standard height) or about 2.9 m (high cube). Work at this height is subject to occupational safety rules; in a commercial context this applies explicitly (see [Occupational safety around site containers](/en/ratgeber/container-arbeitsschutz-baustelle)). Even in private use you should only work with a ladder, protection and dry weather, and only step on the sheet using load-spreading boards. Scratching the roof opens the door to rust yourself.

## Typical mistakes

- **Waterproofing without finding the cause.** A roof coat does not cure condensation.
- **Painting straight onto rust.** Without removing rust and priming, the coating comes off together with the rust.
- **Too many holes.** Every screw without sealing is a leak. If you have to drill, seal each spot individually and document it.
- **Just weighing the roof down.** Extra load without consulting the manufacturer or an engineer, and without checking whether the build-up is relevant under building law.
- **Forgetting snow.** Especially with extra build-ups it adds to the dead load.

## Important note

This article is general orientation and replaces neither a structural calculation nor the manufacturer's information. Whether a roof build-up or change of use needs permission depends on location, duration and use; the basics are in [Containers and building permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung). Under German law, building regulation is a matter for the federal states and follows the state building codes. Cost figures are orientation values without guarantee.

## Conclusion

The container roof is robust but not a given: it only drains reliably if the container stands level and the sheet is intact, and it takes area loads only after checking. First establish whether it is a leak or condensation, prepare the substrate thoroughly and keep the roof free of water and dirt. If you plan more than a coating, check before buying whether the container is suitable; the checklist in [Buying a used container](/en/ratgeber/gebrauchten-container-kaufen) helps.
