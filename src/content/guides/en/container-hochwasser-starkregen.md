## Why steel is no reason to relax

A container withstands wind, rain and frost – but it is not built to resist water from below or from the side. The floor is plywood on steel cross-members, the door seals are designed for spray rather than pressurised water, and an empty steel box is surprisingly light. Three problems typically occur together in a flood: water gets in and damages contents and floor, the current pushes or shifts the container, and in the worst case buoyancy lifts it. A displaced or floating container is also a hazard to neighbouring land, bridges and fences.

This guide helps you classify the situation and weigh up measures. It replaces neither the authority's advice nor a structural assessment. General questions on the base are covered in [Container foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament). The legal references relate to Germany.

## Three water hazards, three different consequences

"Flood" is not the same as "heavy rain". The measures differ depending on where the water comes from.

| Hazard | Typical pattern | Particularly critical for the container | First step |
|---|---|---|---|
| River flood | Water level rises over hours to days, current, floating debris | Buoyancy, flow pressure, displacement, contamination | Check the location in the flood hazard map, ask the authority |
| Heavy rain / flash flood | Short-term, often away from watercourses, water collects in hollows and on slopes | Ponding on the uphill side, undermining, flooding in depressions | Read the terrain: where does the water flow, where does it pond? |
| Groundwater / backflow | Slow rise, water from below, sewer pushes back | Moisture in the floor, backflow via connections | Secure connections, plan backflow-proof pipework |

So when assessing a plot, do not just ask "is there a river nearby?". A hollow in an open field or a position at the foot of a slope can also be a danger spot in heavy rain. Hints come from the federal states' flood hazard maps, in some cases municipal heavy-rain hazard maps, and from local knowledge of residents and the building authority. Whether such maps exist for your site, and in what detail, varies by region.

## Worked example: when does a container float?

According to Archimedes, buoyancy equals the weight of the displaced water. For a rough estimate we take a 20-foot standard container measuring about 6.058 × 2.438 m outside. The footprint is roughly 14.8 m². We assume a tare weight of about 2,250 kg; see also [Sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse).

With water at height h around the container, the maximum buoyancy – on the hypothetical assumption that the container is completely watertight – is approximately footprint × h × 1,000 kg/m³:

| Water depth around the container | Calculated maximum buoyancy | Surplus over tare weight of 2,250 kg |
|---|---|---|
| 10 cm | approx. 1,480 kg | no floating |
| 20 cm | approx. 2,950 kg | approx. 700 kg |
| 30 cm | approx. 4,430 kg | approx. 2,180 kg |
| 50 cm | approx. 7,390 kg | approx. 5,140 kg |
| 100 cm | approx. 14,770 kg | approx. 12,520 kg |

The draught of the empty container is 2,250 kg / (14.8 m² × 1,000 kg/m³) ≈ 0.15 m. From a water depth of around 15 cm, a completely watertight, empty container would theoretically be able to float. A fully loaded container with a gross weight of about 30,250 kg (tare plus approx. 28,000 kg payload) would, by calculation, need a little over 2 m of water around it.

Three conclusions follow:

1. **Loading matters.** With 3,000 kg of contents (gross weight approx. 5,250 kg), a surplus of about 2,140 kg would still remain at 50 cm of water; with 5,200 kg of contents, the weight at that depth would already exceed the maximum buoyancy.
2. **The figure is an upper limit.** Real containers are not watertight: door seals, vents and later cut-outs let water in, which reduces buoyancy. In return there is water inside, and the contents are damaged. The calculation shows the order of magnitude, not the moment of floating.
3. **Contents are not a safeguard.** Relying on the weight of the goods is unreliable, since storage gets emptied, rearranged or cleared on warning. Protection against displacement must work independently of the contents.

Flow forces in moving water come on top. They cannot be stated as a flat figure because they depend on flow velocity, water depth and the area exposed to the flow – a professional assessment is needed here.

## Siting: comparing the concepts

Which approach makes sense depends on the hazard and the use.

| Concept | Basic idea | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relocate | Choose a flood-free area | Removes the problem at its root | Not always possible, plot layout |
| Raised siting | Container on foundations or supports above the expected water level | Contents stay dry, water can pass beneath | Access, ramp, structural design, planning law, cost |
| Anchoring to the foundation | Connect the container to the foundation (professional design) | Protection against displacement and floating | Anchoring does not replace checking the water levels |
| Mobile use | Site the container only when needed or move it on warning | Low investment | Reaction time, crane/truck must be available |
| Keep contents flood-free | Valuables on shelving, pallets or not in the container at all | Limits the damage | The container itself remains at risk |

Important: raised or anchored siting is a **structural intervention** and may be assessed differently under planning law than a container simply placed on the ground. This applies particularly in designated flood zones. There, besides the state building code, water law applies (the federal Water Resources Act, WHG, plus state water acts); erecting structures there is in principle restricted, with exceptions possible under conditions. Storing items that the water could carry away may also be regulated. Whether your project needs a permit depends on location, duration and use – see the guide [Building permit for containers](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung) and, for rural-area plots, [Development plan and outlying areas](/en/ratgeber/container-bebauungsplan-aussenbereich).

## Heavy rain: reading the terrain instead of flat rules

In heavy rain, micro-topography often decides. Practical questions before siting:

- Is the site in a hollow, a cutting or at the foot of a slope where water converges?
- Where would surface water flow if the container stands in its way? Does this put more load on a neighbouring plot? (This can be relevant under civil law, see [Neighbour law and boundary distances](/en/ratgeber/container-nachbarrecht-grenzabstand).)
- Can water flow beneath the container and drain away, or does it pond there? Free ventilation underneath and drainage help; compare [Foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).
- Is there a risk of the support points being undermined by strong runoff?
- Are access routes or paths themselves flooded in heavy rain (emergency access, evacuation)?

An access ramp or a small kerb at the entrance does not replace flood planning. In short heavy rain with low water depth, however, it can reduce water entering. How high protection must be is determined by the locally expected water level, not by a rule of thumb.

## Electrics, connections and technical equipment

Water and electricity do not mix. So plan for:

- **Distribution board, sockets and appliances raised**, as far as the qualified electrician provides for it. More in [Electrical planning for the interior fit-out](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau) and [Power connection](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss).
- **Do not switch on after a flood.** A qualified electrician must inspect cables, distribution board and appliances before power is restored.
- **Mind backflow:** Where a sanitary container is connected to the sewer, wastewater can push back in heavy rain. Backflow protection and what lies above the backflow level belong in the plan; compare [Water connection and wastewater](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser).
- **Do not store hazardous materials in the danger zone.** Batteries, chemicals and fuels can leak or react in a flood. The general principles are in [Storing batteries and hazardous materials in a container](/en/ratgeber/container-akkus-gefahrstoffe-lagern).

## What to do after a flood

The container itself survives water better than its interior, but not without consequences:

1. Safety first: enter the container and surroundings only once power is off and structural stability is not in doubt.
2. Photograph the damage before clearing up; this makes settlement easier.
3. Remove mud and moisture, dry and ventilate thoroughly. The plywood floor soaks up water and can swell or rot; see [Repairing or replacing the floor](/en/ratgeber/container-boden-sanieren-austauschen).
4. Check paint damage and incipient rust and treat early, see [Care and rust protection](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz).
5. Check stability: are supports undermined, has the container settled or shifted? If in doubt, call a specialist firm.

## Insurance and responsibility

Storm, fire and burglary are included in many policies, whereas flooding, backflow and heavy rain are often only available as an **add-on** (natural hazards). Whether a container and its contents are covered, whether cover is offered at your location and whether waiting periods apply is for the insurer alone to say. Details are in [Insuring a container](/en/ratgeber/container-versichern). For a rented container, also check who bears the risk under the contract – more in [Rental contract and law](/en/ratgeber/container-mietvertrag-recht).

## Checklist before siting

- [ ] Hazard checked: flood hazard map, heavy-rain information, enquiry with the municipality
- [ ] Designated flood zone? If so: ask the authority before any measure
- [ ] Terrain read: hollows, slope foot, flow paths
- [ ] Siting height and securing agreed with a specialist firm or structural engineer
- [ ] Securing independent of the contents
- [ ] Electrics raised and planned by a qualified electrician
- [ ] Valuables and hazardous materials kept out of the danger zone
- [ ] Natural-hazard cover clarified with the insurer
- [ ] Emergency plan: who reacts to an official warning, how is the site cleared?

## Important notice

> This article gives general guidance. The calculated values are simplified rough estimates and not a structural proof. Flood and inundation areas, conditions and responsibilities in Germany depend on the federal state and municipality (state building code, water law under the WHG and state water acts, and in rural areas additionally section 35 BauGB). Clarify location, permit requirements and securing with the competent authority, a structural engineer or specialist firm and your insurer.

## Conclusion

Against water, the main remedy for a container is not to stand where water ponds or flows. Where that is impossible, three things matter: assess the site realistically, raise the siting and secure it independently of the contents, and protect electrics, hazardous materials and valuables from the water. The calculation shows that even a small water depth could theoretically float an empty container – a reason not to treat buoyancy as a side issue. And because measures in flood zones are sensitive under planning and water law, the conversation with the authority and the insurer belongs at the start of planning, not the end.
