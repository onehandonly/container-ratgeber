The choice between a self-build and a turnkey purchase is usually made earlier than people expect – at the moment the container is ordered. A bare module that a supplier will later fit out has to meet different requirements than one you plan to convert yourself over two years. Get that decision wrong and you pay twice.

This guide compares the three realistic routes, names the skills a self-build demands, and shows what a turnkey price normally excludes. The individual cost items are covered in [Container tiny house: costs](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten) and [Fit-out costs by trade](/en/ratgeber/container-ausbaukosten-gewerke), the structural basics in [Tiny house from a container](/en/ratgeber/tiny-house-aus-container). How container modules differ from prefabricated and masonry houses in principle is explained in [Modular build, prefab or solid construction](/en/ratgeber/modulbau-fertighaus-massivbau-vergleich).

> **Note on all prices:** All amounts are rough **approximate ranges** for orientation (as of 2026). They depend heavily on region, specification, own labour and market conditions, and constitute neither an offer nor a price guarantee. Obtain several current quotes for every project.

## The three routes at a glance

Between "do everything yourself" and "buy everything" sits a middle path that works best in practice.

**Route 1 – Full self-build.** You buy a used or new container and handle planning, insulation, drylining, floors, furniture and finishes yourself. Electrics and plumbing are connected by trades; the rest happens on evenings and weekends.

**Route 2 – Partial fit-out with trades.** You buy a bare module, ideally with window and door openings already cut and structurally reinforced at the factory. Individual trades – insulation, electrics, plumbing, heating – are contracted separately, while you do all or part of the interior work.

**Route 3 – Turnkey from a supplier.** A manufacturer delivers a finished module with a defined energy standard, electrical and plumbing installation, floor finishes and sometimes a kitchen. You provide the plot, foundation and service connections.

| Criterion | Self-build | Partial fit-out | Turnkey |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total cost (approx., 20 ft) | €21,000–40,000 | €30,000–50,000 | €40,000–65,000 |
| Total cost (approx., 40 ft) | €32,000–60,000 | €45,000–75,000 | €60,000–90,000 |
| Own labour required | approx. 530–1,140 hrs | approx. 200–500 hrs | approx. 20–60 hrs (coordination) |
| Time to move-in | 12–30 months | 8–15 months | 4–9 months |
| Know-how required | high | medium | low |
| Warranty | materials and individually commissioned work only | per trade, separately | on the complete works |
| Risk of defects | largely yours | interface disputes possible | with the supplier |
| Bank financing | difficult | moderate | most feasible |

**What the total cost includes:** container or module, fit-out, foundation, transport with crane, and design, structural engineering and the building application. **Not included** are the plot, utility connections (approx. €3,000–15,000) and external works. The self-build and turnkey figures match the worked examples in [Container tiny house: costs](/en/ratgeber/container-tiny-house-kosten); the self-build hours come from the trade table further down.

The spread is driven less by the construction method than by the specification. A self-build with high-grade insulation, triple glazing and a heat pump quickly reaches the price of a basic turnkey module.

## Self-build: what you really need to be able to do

Self-builds rarely fail on motivation. They fail on three things: structure, building physics and stamina.

### Structure when cutting openings

The load-bearing structure of a shipping container is a steel frame of four corner posts plus bottom and top side rails; the eight corner castings are its nodes. The corrugated walls brace this frame and, in the long walls, also help carry loads – they are not merely weather protection. Every opening for a window or door interrupts that wall panel and weakens the bracing, considerably so for large openings in a long wall. After cutting, the opening must be framed with steel sections and the wall's stiffness structurally replaced – for larger openings, structural calculations are a condition of approval, not an option. Having the openings cut at the factory or by a metalwork firm removes the single largest risk from the project for comparatively little money. The process and typical mistakes are described in [Retrofitting windows and doors](/en/ratgeber/container-fenster-tueren-nachruesten).

### Building physics and moisture

Steel is a continuous thermal bridge. Without a properly planned vapour control layer and an insulation build-up that pushes the dew point out of the construction, condensation forms on the inside of the steel skin – invisible behind the lining, with mould and corrosion as the result. This mistake is particularly expensive because it often only shows after one or two winters, when the lining has to come off again. Where the critical spots are is explained in [Thermal bridges in containers](/en/ratgeber/waermebruecken-container); the insulation build-up itself in [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen).

### Electrics and plumbing belong to certified trades

In Germany, connecting to the public grid and commissioning a customer installation may only be carried out by an electrical contractor listed in a network operator's register of installers. Something similar applies to the drinking water installation behind the service connection: installing or substantially altering it is reserved for the water supplier or an installation firm registered with it. Even if you are handy, clarify early which preparatory work the contractor will accept – many will not take responsibility for cables they did not lay themselves. Preparing conduits, chases and battens in agreement with the contractor is usually possible. For planning circuits see [Electrical planning for the fit-out](/en/ratgeber/container-elektroplanung-innenausbau).

### Realistic time requirement

| Trade | Time (approx., 20 ft) | DIY feasible? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planning, permits | 60–150 hrs | yes, with a planner |
| Rust protection, prep | 30–60 hrs | yes |
| Openings + reinforcement | 40–80 hrs | only with structural proof |
| Insulation, vapour barrier | 80–150 hrs | yes, with care |
| Drylining, floors | 120–250 hrs | yes |
| Electrics (prep/connection) | 40–80 hrs | partly |
| Plumbing, heating | 60–120 hrs | partly |
| Kitchen, furniture, finish | 100–250 hrs | yes |
| **Total** | **approx. 530–1,140 hrs** | |

Add 30 to 50 per cent to every estimate you make yourself – the table describes brisk work without rework. At ten hours a week, an 800-hour project takes 80 weeks, around a year and a half.

## Worked example: what is your own labour worth?

The question "Is a self-build worth it?" can be boiled down to one number: the saving per hour of your own work. The basis is the 20 ft total cost from the overview table (excluding plot, utility connections and external works).

| Figure | Self-build | Turnkey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total cost (range) | approx. €21,000–40,000 | approx. €40,000–65,000 |
| Midpoint of the range | approx. €30,500 | approx. €52,500 |
| Own labour (midpoint of 530–1,140 hrs) | approx. 835 hrs | approx. 40 hrs |

- **Saving at the midpoints:** €52,500 − €30,500 = **approx. €22,000**, around 42 per cent of the turnkey midpoint.
- **Saving per hour:** €22,000 ÷ 835 hrs ≈ **approx. €26 per hour** of own labour.
- **Full range:** comparing the ranges honestly gives a saving between €0 (an expensive self-build at €40,000 against a cheap module at €40,000) and €44,000 (a cheap self-build at €21,000 against an expensive module at €65,000).

The roughly €26 per hour is money you do not spend – so it is net, not gross. Whether that is worthwhile depends on what you could earn in the same time or how much you value the work itself. Two effects shift the sum against the self-build: the 30 to 50 per cent time overrun (with 50 per cent more hours the value drops to around €18 per hour) and the cost of rework if a mistake only shows after the first winter. Using second-hand components or deliberately economising on fittings works in the self-build's favour.

## Turnkey: what is in the price – and what is not

"Turnkey" is not a protected term. In practice it almost always describes the module ex works, not a habitable house on your plot.

| Item | Usually included in the module price | Usually extra (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Module, insulation, windows | yes | – |
| Interior, bathroom, electrics | yes | – |
| Kitchen | sometimes | €2,000–9,000 |
| Foundation | no | €600–8,000 (pad foundations to slab) |
| Transport and crane | no | €350–800 per trip locally with a crane-mounted lorry; for long delivery from the factory or a mobile crane approx. €1,500–4,000 |
| Service connections (power, water, sewer) | no | €3,000–15,000 |
| Permit design, structural engineering, application | no | €1,500–6,000 |
| External works, terrace, access | no | €2,000–15,000 |

### Example: from module price to move-in price

A turnkey 20 ft module is offered at €50,000 ex works, kitchen included. On a plot with utilities close by, the following are added:

| Item | Amount (example) |
| --- | --- |
| Module ex works | €50,000 |
| Strip foundation | €3,000 |
| Transport from factory and crane | €2,000 |
| Design, structural engineering, application | €3,000 |
| Service connections | €6,000 |
| Simple terrace and path | €3,000 |
| **Total** | **€67,000** |

The ancillary costs here are €17,000, or 34 per cent of the module price. Without utility connections and external works – on the same basis as the overview table – the example comes to €58,000. With expensive servicing, complex foundations or a crane with a long reach, 50 per cent or more is possible. A quote is therefore only comparable if you ask every supplier about these items; see [Comparing quotes and negotiating](/en/ratgeber/container-angebote-vergleichen).

## Choosing a supplier

Because we are independent, we name no companies – but we do name supplier types and the criteria that distinguish them.

| Supplier type | Strength | Typical weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Modular manufacturers (series) | price, delivery reliability, documentation | little customisation |
| Regional fit-out firms | adaptability, short distances | capacity, schedule risk |
| Trading / brokerage platforms | wide choice | unclear liability for defects |
| Imports without a local entity | low module price | code compliance, warranty hard to enforce |
| Architect + individual trades | design quality | higher cost, coordination effort |

**Assessment criteria:**

- **References** you are allowed to visit – ideally a building that has survived at least two winters.
- **Workshop visit**: see how insulating and welding are done. A supplier who will not show production usually has a reason.
- **Structural proof** for openings, stacking and anchoring, in a form your authority can review.
- **Energy standard with numbers**: U-values for wall, floor, roof and windows, not "very well insulated".
- **Warranty**: scope, duration and who carries it – manufacturer or broker.
- **Payment plan** tied to construction progress, not to the calendar.
- **Handover** with a written protocol, defects list and retention.

### Warning signs

- Large advance payments (more than 20–30 per cent) before work starts – paying everything up front is especially risky, see [Fake container dealers](/en/ratgeber/fake-containerhaendler-vorkasse-betrug).
- No fixed prices, only "guide prices" subject to adjustment.
- Missing structural, U-value or material documentation when asked.
- No local contracting entity, or frequently changing company names.
- Pressure through time-limited discounts and "sign today".
- References shown only as renderings, never as real buildings.

## Contract checklist

- Complete **specification of works** as part of the contract, not just a brochure
- **Fixed price** with clearly named exclusions and a validity period
- **Delivery date** binding, with agreed consequences for delay
- **Payment plan** linked to milestones, final instalment only after handover
- **Interfaces** defined: who provides foundation, crane, connections?
- **Warranty periods** and named points of contact
- **Securities**: performance security or retention of around five per cent (see below)
- **Variations** in writing only, with pre-calculated prices
- **Termination and withdrawal rights** if permission is refused
- **Delivery and handover protocol** agreed, including a check for transport damage – see [Delivery and handover protocol](/en/ratgeber/container-anlieferung-abnahmeprotokoll)

### Sales contract or construction contract?

Which protections you have as a private individual under German law depends on how the contract is classified. If a finished module is simply delivered, it is more likely a sale (or a contract for the supply of goods to be produced) under sales law. If the supplier erects a new building on your plot, it may be a **consumer construction contract** (§ 650i BGB). Additional rules then apply, including a mandatory building specification, a right of withdrawal, a cap on instalments at 90 per cent of the total price and a right to security of five per cent (§ 650m BGB). The classification in an individual case is not trivial – have the contract checked before signing, for example by a consumer advice centre or a lawyer specialising in construction law. Background on defect rights when buying is in [Purchase contract and warranty](/en/ratgeber/container-kaufvertrag-gewaehrleistung).

## Example schedule

| Phase | Self-build | Turnkey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plot, pre-check with authority | month 1–3 | month 1–3 |
| Design, structure, application | month 3–8 | month 2–6 |
| Procurement / manufacture | month 8–10 | month 6–9 |
| Foundation, servicing | month 10–13 | month 7–9 |
| Fit-out / installation | month 13–28 | month 9–10 |
| Handover, move-in | month 30 | month 10 |

The schedule counts from the first pre-check with the building authority and therefore sits at the upper end of the ranges in the overview table – for turnkey, slightly above it. The permit phase is equally long for both routes and is often the biggest uncertainty: whether and under which procedure a container home needs permission depends on location, duration and use – see [Container building permit](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

## Important note

This guide provides general orientation based on the situation in Germany and does not replace legal, tax or specialist design advice. Cost figures are guide values without guarantee. Structural design, energy certification and permit eligibility must be checked for each project by qualified professionals; the relevant state building code and the requirements of the local authority are decisive.

## Conclusion

A self-build pays off if you are confident with tools, can commit time reliably over one to two years, and will have structure and building physics verified externally. In the worked example it saves around €22,000 or roughly 42 per cent against turnkey at the midpoints – about €26 per hour of own labour, bought with the execution risk and no warranty on the completed works.

Turnkey is right when schedule, financing and liability for defects matter more than price. What decides the outcome is not the supplier's name but the contract: fixed price, verifiable documentation, milestone payments and a proper handover. Work from the module price to the move-in price – in the example, 34 per cent in ancillary costs was added.

For most clients the partial fit-out is the pragmatic compromise: a bare module with factory-cut openings and structural proof, certified trades for electrics, plumbing and insulation, own labour for interior work and finishes.
