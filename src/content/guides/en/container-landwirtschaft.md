Farms constantly need extra robust, lockable space: for spare parts, operating supplies, seasonal goods or direct sales. A container looks like an obvious answer – it is weatherproof, theft-resistant, relocatable and quickly available. Yet farmyards and fields are demanding surroundings for steel boxes: large temperature swings, damp, dust, rodents, aggressive substances and often soft ground. This guide shows what a container is good for, what it is less suited to, and what you should clarify under planning law before buying. The legal remarks refer to Germany.

## What containers are really good for on the farm

Suitability depends less on the container than on the **stored goods**. The key questions: can the goods tolerate fluctuating temperature and humidity, and is it dangerous if they leak, burn or are stolen?

| Use | Suitability | What matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tools, spare parts, accessories, implements | good | shelving, lockability, lighting; damp only matters for rust-prone metal parts |
| Packaging, pallets, crates, nets | good | keep dry, watch for rodents |
| Seed, feed, dry produce | limited | ventilation and insulation needed, otherwise condensation and mould; rodent protection |
| Fresh crops, potatoes, fruit | usually only as a refrigerated container | temperature control needed, see the [reefer guide](/en/ratgeber/kuehlcontainer-ratgeber) |
| Diesel, oils, lubricants | only in a suitable design | drip tray and water-protection requirements; an ordinary storage container is generally not enough |
| Plant protection products, certain fertilisers | only after clarifying the requirements | specialist legal rules, possibly a dedicated hazardous-goods container |
| Farm shop, sales stall | possible | permit, hygiene, windows/serving opening, power |
| Break or rest room | possible | insulation, ventilation, occupational safety, possibly sanitary facilities |
| Accommodation for seasonal workers | only with considerable effort | minimum standards for accommodation, building law |

For most farms the greatest benefit is a **dry tool and parts store** – exactly the use where a simple second-hand container can hardly go wrong. For the types, see [storage containers](/en/container/lagercontainer) and the [storage container guide](/en/ratgeber/lagercontainer-ratgeber).

## The moisture problem: why grain and seed are demanding

A steel box that heats up in the sun by day and cools at night is a condenser. Warm, humid air condenses on the cold inner surfaces, and with organic goods the moisture also comes from the goods themselves. The consequences: dripping from the ceiling, damp stored goods, mould and rust from the inside. An uninsulated, unventilated container is therefore generally **not a good choice** for moisture-sensitive goods such as seed, feed or dried produce.

If it has to be a container, these measures help:

- insulation and ventilation following the principles in [Insulating a container](/en/ratgeber/container-daemmen) and [Container ventilation](/en/ratgeber/container-belueftung)
- store goods dry only, in crates or on pallets, never directly against the steel wall
- lift the floor off the ground so that no moisture rises from below ([foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament))
- inspect regularly – a container is no substitute for a climate-controlled store

## Worked example: how much space does a 20 ft container offer?

The interior of a standard 20 ft container measures approx. 5.90 × 2.35 m, around 13.9 m² of floor area (5.90 × 2.35 = 13.87). The high cube has the same floor area but more height (approx. 2.70 instead of 2.39 m).

If you plan a shelved parts store, floor area is lost to aisles. With 0.5 m deep shelves on both long sides, 2.35 − 1.00 = 1.35 m of aisle remains. The shelving per side is then about 5.9 m long × 0.5 m deep. That is a guide figure: the door area, the door leaves and light fittings reduce the usable length.

If you only want to park equipment now and then, you plan no aisle but use the door opening (approx. 2.34 m wide) as the way in and out for small machines. Check beforehand whether something fits, using the dimensions in [Container sizes and dimensions](/en/ratgeber/container-groessen-und-masse): the door opening, at approx. 2.34 × 2.28 m (high cube approx. 2.58 m high), is considerably tighter than the external dimensions suggest. A tractor with a cab will usually not fit. If you want to drive equipment in and out, consider an [open-side container](/en/container/open-side-container).

## Planning law: the link to the business decides in the outer zone

If the farm lies within a built-up area or within a development plan, the usual state building code and plan rules apply. It is different for farmsteads and land in the **Aussenbereich** (outer zone), which in principle is to be kept free of development. Section 35 of the Federal Building Code (BauGB) governs the outer zone.

Projects that **serve an agricultural or forestry business** can be permissible there as privileged uses. That does not mean every container in a field is allowed. In practice the building authority typically examines:

- Is there actually an agricultural business that is established for the long term? A hobby or pure pastime generally does not meet this requirement.
- Does the container serve the business – or is it merely a pretext for another use?
- Is it subordinate to the business, i.e. does it fit the business in size and purpose?
- Do nature conservation, landscape, water protection or distances to woodland and watercourses stand in the way?

Whether a container needs no building permit is regulated differently by the state building codes. Exemption from the permit procedure also does not replace planning-law permissibility. For details see [Development plan and outer zone](/en/ratgeber/container-bebauungsplan-aussenbereich) and [Containers and building permission](/en/ratgeber/container-baugenehmigung).

Changes of use are especially delicate: a tool shed quickly becomes a rest or sales room, a store becomes accommodation. Each of these uses can trigger different requirements, see [Building application and change of use](/en/ratgeber/container-bauantrag-nutzungsaenderung).

## Farm shop and direct marketing

A container as a point of sale for your own produce is common. Whether it counts under planning law as part of the agricultural business depends on the individual case, for instance on its size and on whether only or mainly your own products are sold. Depending on the range, food law, hygiene rules, power and water connections and requirements for customer access and safety also apply. Helpful are [Power supply](/en/ratgeber/container-stromanschluss), [Water supply and wastewater](/en/ratgeber/container-wasseranschluss-abwasser) and the notes on sales structures in [Containers for events and catering](/en/ratgeber/container-fuer-events-und-gastronomie). Ask the building authority and the food inspection office before you buy.

## Hazardous substances, fuel and fertiliser

Here the rule is: do not improvise. Anyone storing diesel, oils, plant protection products or certain fertilisers is subject, depending on quantity and substance, to hazardous-substances, plant-protection and water law. For water law, the Federal Water Resources Act, the ordinance on facilities handling water-polluting substances and the state water acts apply. An ordinary storage container has no drip tray, no ventilation concept and no fire-protection concept. Special designs exist for such substances, see [hazardous-goods containers](/en/container/gefahrstoffcontainer). Clarify the requirements beforehand with the agricultural chamber or plant protection service, the water authority and the fire protection authority.

## Siting in the field and farmyard

- **Ground:** on soft ground the container settles unevenly. Doors jam and the frame distorts. Gravel and pad footings are the usual answer, see [Container foundation](/en/ratgeber/container-fundament).
- **Access:** check whether a lorry with a crane or a tilting hook loader can reach the site, and what the ground and load-bearing capacity of the approach are like. Costs and technique: [Transport and delivery costs](/en/ratgeber/container-transport-lieferkosten).
- **Moving with your own equipment:** a container is lifted by the corner fittings or with fork pockets and suitable lifting gear, not with improvised chains on the sheet metal. See [Lifting a container correctly](/en/ratgeber/container-richtig-anheben).
- **Theft:** remote sites are at risk. Advice in [Securing a container](/en/ratgeber/container-sichern-diebstahlschutz).
- **Rodents and animals:** secure gaps at door seals and vents with fine-mesh grilles.
- **Rust:** on the farm, damp, fertiliser and stable fumes attack the paint. Regular care following [Care and rust protection](/en/ratgeber/container-pflege-und-rostschutz).

The floor of a sea container is plywood on steel cross members. Spread heavy point loads, such as barrels or machinery on small footprints, onto boards. How much the floor carries is covered in [Loading a container correctly](/en/ratgeber/container-richtig-beladen).

## Checklist before buying

1. Write down the stored goods and their requirements (temperature, humidity, hazardous substance).
2. Determine the location: built-up area, development plan or outer zone?
3. In the outer zone, agree the link to the business and the form of business with the building authority.
4. Choose the type: standard, high cube, open side or special design.
5. Plan the foundation and access.
6. Allow for ventilation, possibly insulation, and power.
7. Clarify security and insurance.
8. For hazardous substances, involve the responsible bodies before you buy.

## Important note

This guide is orientation, not legal, tax or professional advice. Building law is state law (state building codes) and, in the outer zone, additionally federal law (BauGB); permissibility depends on location, duration and use. Cost and dimension figures are approximate values without guarantee. For a specific project, contact the building authority, the agricultural chamber or the competent specialist office and, where appropriate, a tax adviser.

## Conclusion

On the farm, containers are a good store for tools, parts and packaging. For moisture-sensitive goods, hazardous substances and any form of occupancy or sales, you need a specification and permission that match the use. In the outer zone, a demonstrable link to the business is the crux.
